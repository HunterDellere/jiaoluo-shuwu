//角落書屋/Slices/Ordering Food
饱	bǎo	full, satisfied, to eat one's fill — The food radical 饣 wraps the sound of 包 (bāo), so the glyph itself is a stomach packed full like a dumpling.
菜	cài	vegetable, dish, cuisine — Plants gathered to be eaten. From the picked greens grows a whole table: the vegetable, the cooked dish, the regional cuisine, and the slangy put-down for anyone weak at a task.
茶	chá	tea — The character that gave the world its word for tea: a grass over wood over man, a plant that shaped civilization, and the locus of China's richest material culture.
吃	chī	to eat — To eat, and the extraordinary range of Chinese life encoded in a single food verb.
蛋	dàn	egg, egg-shaped thing, scoundrel — A pictophonetic egg: the 虫 radical for small creatures sits under a sound element, and in slang it turns people into scoundrels.
点	diǎn	dot, point, a little, o'clock — From a single dot of ink the character spreads into points and decimals, into the hours of the clock, into ordering food and lighting a flame, and into the small quantity that softens a sentence.
馆	guǎn	a public building, hall, shop, establishment — A food-radical building: the eating-house that grew into the library, the museum, and the embassy.
喝	hē	to drink; (hè) to shout, to cry out — A mouth radical paired with the phonetic 曷, splitting cleanly into the everyday hē you drink with and the older hè you shout with.
鸡	jī	chicken, fowl, rooster — The simplified 鸡 swaps a chicken-coop hand 又 in where the traditional 雞 kept the sound-bearer 奚 beside the bird.
苦	kǔ	bitter, hardship, suffering, painstaking — The grass radical over 古 names a bitter herb on the tongue, then stretches to every flavor of hardship and effort.
面	miàn	face, surface, side — A pictograph of a face inside its outline that opened outward into surfaces, sides, and aspects, became a measure word for flat things, and in simplified script swallowed the separate character for flour and noodles.
牛	niú	ox, cow, cattle; (slang) awesome — A four-stroke pictograph of an ox head seen straight on, the horns still visible; in modern slang it means impressively good.
肉	ròu	meat, flesh, the pulp of fruit — A pictograph of a meat slab with its marbling drawn inside; squeezed onto the left of a character it becomes 月, the flesh-moon of the body radicals.
水	shuǐ	water — The first of the five elements, the radical that runs through hundreds of compounds, and the Daoist image of how to act in the world.
香	xiāng	fragrant, delicious, incense, popular — Grain stacked over a mouth of sweetness: 香 is the smell of ripe rice, and the word still means both perfume and a hot meal worth eating.
茶道	chá dào	the way of tea — From wild forest leaf to civilizational ritual: how tea became China's quintessential art of presence.
茶具	chá ju	Tea Implements — The vessels, tools, and objects of the tea table: from the Yixing purple clay teapot to the fairness pitcher, each implement with a name, a function, and an aesthetic logic.
风水	fēng shuǐ	wind and water — Geomancy, qi flow, and the cosmological logic of favorable siting.
工夫茶	gōng fu chá	Gongfu Tea — Tea prepared with skill and time: the Southern Chinese brewing method that defines serious tea culture, built on small vessels, multiple short steeps, and total attention.
面子	miàn zi	face, honor — Giving, losing, and saving face: the social grammar of dignity.
山水	shān shuǐ	mountains and water; the landscape — The paired word that names the Chinese landscape: not "scenery" in the Western sense, but a cosmological pairing of the still and the flowing that became the central subject of a thousand years of painting and poetry.
山水画	shān shuǐ huà	Chinese Landscape Painting — Mountains and waters as a cosmological pair -- the genre that made ink the equal of philosophy.
饱经风霜	bǎo jīng fēng shuāng	to have weathered wind and frost: hardened by hardship — 饱 here means having had a full share of something. The image is a face lined by years of exposure; used of people who have lived through long adversity. Common in literary and journalistic prose.
饱食终日	bǎo shí zhōng rì	to eat one's fill all day: to idle in comfort — From the Analects (论语), where Confucius reproaches those who eat their fill the whole day and apply their minds to nothing. A pointed criticism of idleness; carries a moral edge.
饱学之士	bǎo xu é zhī shì	a scholar full of learning: an erudite person — 饱 extends from a full stomach to a full mind. 饱学 means saturated with learning; a respectful term for a deeply read scholar. Formal register.
饱以老拳	bǎo yǐ lǎo quán	to treat to a full helping of fists: to give a sound beating — A grim joke on the eating sense: instead of a meal, the recipient gets a generous serving of punches. From the Jìn Shū (晋书). Vivid and slightly humorous in tone.
本来面目	běn lái miàn mù	original face: a thing's true, unaltered nature — Lit: original-face-and-features. A Chan Buddhist phrase for one's true self before all conditioning, now used broadly for the real character of anything once the disguises are stripped away.
别馆离宫	bié guǎn lí gōng	separate lodges and detached palaces: the same scattered residences, with the lodge named first — The attested inversion of 离宫别馆, listed as its own form in idiom dictionaries. Putting 馆 first foregrounds the lodging over the palace.
兵无常势，水无常形。	bīng wú cháng shì , shuǐ wú cháng xíng .	An army has no fixed configuration, as water has no fixed shape. — Sunzi's image for strategic adaptability, strikingly close to the water imagery of the Daodejing.
茶馆酒肆	chá guǎn jiǔ sì	teahouses and wine-shops: the everyday public houses of a town — A four-character set phrase pairing 茶馆 with 酒肆 (the wine-shop) to stand for the street-level places where ordinary people gathered, drank, and talked.
沉鱼落雁	chén yú luò yàn	"sinking fish and falling geese" — describing a woman so beautiful that fish dive and birds fall when she passes — From the Zhuangzi, originally a wry critique of relativism in beauty (the fish do not actually find her beautiful , they hide because they are afraid of humans).
吃喝玩乐	chī hē wán lè	eat, drink, play, be merry: a life of indulgence — Four verbs of pleasure strung together, with 喝 as the drinking. Usually mildly disapproving, said of someone who does nothing but enjoy themselves.
吃苦耐劳	chī kǔ nài láo	to endure hardship and persevere in labor — a core work-ethic virtue — The defining work-ethic phrase in Chinese culture. Applied to individuals, generations, and national character. Eating bitterness + tolerating labor = the formula for achievement.
吃亏是福	chī kuī shì fú	suffering loss is a blessing — short-term disadvantage leads to long-term benefit — A proverb attributed to Zheng Banqiao (Qing dynasty). The Daoist-inflected wisdom: accepting being at a disadvantage cultivates humility and patience, which eventually bring greater good. Widely quoted in Chinese business and parenting contexts.
愁眉苦脸	chóu méi kǔ liǎn	knitted brows and a bitter face: a picture of gloom and worry — 愁 (worry) eyebrows over a 苦 (bitter) face. The whole head sags with anxiety. A vivid, everyday way to say someone looks miserable.
大吃大喝	dà chī dà hē	to eat and drink lavishly: feast extravagantly — The doubled 大 stresses excess. Modern usage often flags wasteful banqueting or living beyond one's means.
大喝一声	dà hè yī shēng	to give a great shout: bellow once, loudly — Here 喝 takes its hè reading, "to shout". A stock phrase in classical fiction and storytelling, the warrior or judge who freezes a room with a single roar.
当头棒喝	dāng tóu bàng hè	a staff-blow and a shout to the head — a jolt that wakes someone up — From Chan pedagogy at its most physical: Master Deshan was famous for the staff, Master Linji for the shout, both deployed to shatter a student's conceptual dithering. Now any sharp warning that snaps a person out of a bad course: a failed audit, a doctor's blunt sentence.
灯红酒绿	dēng hóng jiǔ lǜ	red lamps and green wine: gaudy nightlife — The glow of pleasure quarters, often tinged with disapproval. Describes a city of bars and neon, sometimes implying decadence or excess.
滴水穿石	dī shuǐ chuān shí	dripping water bores through stone: persistence wins — The image of a single drip, repeated for years, drilling a hole in rock. The standard idiom for patient, steady effort overcoming a hard obstacle.
点石成金	diǎn shí chéng jīn	to touch stone and turn it to gold: to transform the ordinary into the priceless — Lit: touch-stone-become-gold. From Daoist immortal lore, the touch that turns base rock to gold. Now used of an editor, teacher, or craftsman who makes something dull suddenly shine.
对牛弹琴	duì niú tán qín	playing the qin to a cow — speaking to someone incapable of understanding — From a Han dynasty text attributed to 牟融 Móu Róng. A musician plays the most refined music for a cow; the cow goes on eating grass. The problem is not the cow's intelligence but the category mismatch: the music is addressed to the wrong kind of listener.
风水轮流转	fēng shuǐ lún liú zhuǎn	feng shui rotates in turn — fortunes change; cycles of luck; the wheel keeps turning — The most common figurative use of 风水. Used to express acceptance of change, comfort in adversity, or (more pointedly) schadenfreude when an opponent's luck runs out. 三十年河东，三十年河西，风水轮流转 (thirty years east of the river, thirty years west , the wheel of fortune keeps turning).
改头换面	gǎi tóu huàn miàn	change the head, swap the face: a superficial makeover — Originally Buddhist, on the soul taking a new body. Now usually pejorative: the form is redone but the substance is unchanged, like a rebranded scam.
高山流水	gāo shān liú shuǐ	high mountains, flowing water — finding someone who truly hears you — The piece Boya played when Ziqi first identified the mountains and rivers in his music. As a chengyu, 高山流水 has two uses: the meeting of a 知音 (someone who understands you perfectly), and more broadly, music or artistry of the very highest order.
古色古香	gǔ sè gǔ xiāng	of antique flavor: redolent of the past in look and feel — Literally "ancient color, ancient scent." Praises a building, room, or object that has a tasteful, antique character, steeped in the atmosphere of an older age. A favorite for describing a classical garden, a tea house, or an old street that has kept its period charm.
骨肉相连	gǔ ròu xiāng lián	bone and flesh joined together: bound by blood kinship — The image is that bone and flesh cannot be pulled apart. Used for the inseparable bond between family members, and figuratively for a homeland and its people.
含辛茹苦	hán xīn rú kǔ	to hold pungency and swallow bitterness: to endure every hardship — 茹 (rú) means to eat. Almost always said of parents, especially mothers, who suffer silently for years to raise their children.
寒窗苦读	hán chuāng kǔ dú	cold window, bitter study — The archetypal image of examination preparation: the scholar sitting through winter nights by a cold window, studying by lamplight, without heat or comfort.
汗牛充栋	hàn niú chōng dòng	to sweat the oxen and fill the rafters: a vast number of books — Coined by the Tang writer Liǔ Zōngyuán. So many volumes that hauling them makes the oxen sweat and stacking them fills the house to the roofbeams. Praise for a great library or prolific output.
好汉不吃眼前亏	hǎo hàn bù chī yǎn qián kuī	a wise fellow avoids a loss he can plainly see coming — Lit: a good-fellow does not eat the loss before his eyes. Folk wisdom that real courage includes the sense to step back from a fight you cannot win, rather than charging in for pride.
喝西北风	hē xī běi fēng	to drink the northwest wind: have nothing to eat, go hungry — A vivid colloquial idiom: with no food, all you can swallow is the cold wind. Said wryly of poverty or a failed livelihood, "我们都去喝西北风了".
鹤立鸡群	hè lì jī qún	a crane standing among chickens: head and shoulders above the rest — The tall white crane towering over a yard of squat fowl. Said of a person whose talent or bearing makes them stand out from an ordinary crowd.
画龙点睛	huà lóng diǎn jīng	drawing a dragon and dotting the eye — the small finishing touch that brings the whole alive — From a Tang anecdote: the painter Zhang Sengyou painted dragons but refused to add eyes; when pressed, he dotted the eyes of two , and they immediately flew off the wall. The dot is what brings the form to life. Used today for the small but decisive move that makes a piece of work click.
鸡蛋里挑骨头	jī dàn lǐ tiāo gǔ tou	to pick bones out of an egg: to nitpick, to find fault where there is none — A vivid colloquial saying: an egg has no bones, so hunting for one means inventing flaws. Used of a critic or boss who complains about everything.
鸡飞蛋打	jī fēi dàn dǎ	the hen flies off and the eggs break: to lose everything, ending up empty-handed — A botched scheme where both the chicken and its eggs are lost. Said when an attempt to grab more leaves you with nothing at all.
鸡飞狗跳	jī fēi gǒu tiào	chickens fly and dogs leap: utter chaos and uproar — A farmyard thrown into panic, hens flapping and dogs bolting. Describes a household or scene in total disorder. Lively and colloquial.
鸡毛蒜皮	jī máo suàn pí	chicken feathers and garlic skins: trivial, petty matters — The kitchen sweepings nobody bothers with. Used to dismiss a quarrel or complaint as not worth the fuss: 别为这点鸡毛蒜皮的事吵架.
家常便饭	jiā cháng biàn fàn	an everyday home meal: a common, unremarkable occurrence — Lit: home-ordinary-simple-meal. Literally plain home cooking, used figuratively for anything that happens so routinely it is no big deal: 加班对他来说是家常便饭 "working late is routine for him."
九牛一毛	jiǔ niú yī máo	"one hair from nine oxen" — a trifling amount; a drop in the bucket — 九牛 (nine oxen) and 一毛 (a single hair). A lone hair plucked from nine whole oxen is utterly negligible against the mass it comes from. The phrase comes from Sima Qian, who wrote that his own death would weigh on the world no more than one hair lost from nine oxen.
救苦救难	jiù kǔ jiù nàn	to relieve suffering and rescue from peril — A Buddhist phrase, the standing epithet of the bodhisattva 观音 (Guānyīn), 救苦救难 (one who relieves the suffering and saves from calamity). Now also figurative for any savior figure.
苦尽甘来	kǔ jìn gān lái	bitterness ends, sweetness comes: hardship gives way to better days — 苦 (bitter) and 甘 (sweet) frame a full turn of fortune. The standard consolation offered to anyone enduring a long hard stretch; the reward is built into the saying.
苦口婆心	kǔ kǒu pó xīn	bitter mouth, grandmotherly heart: to urge earnestly and patiently — Describes someone who repeats hard advice tirelessly out of genuine care, the way an old woman nags her grandchild for their own good.
狼吞虎咽	láng tūn hǔ yàn	to wolf down food — to eat ravenously like a wolf and tiger — Lit: wolf-swallow tiger-gulp. Vivid image of eating without manners or restraint. Used humorously for someone who is very hungry or eating too fast.
离宫别馆	lí gōng bié guǎn	detached palaces and separate lodges: the many residences a ruler kept beyond the main palace — A classical 成语 for the scattered country retreats and travelling-lodges of an emperor or great house. Here 馆 carries its oldest sense, the official lodge where the powerful stayed on the road.
怜香惜玉	lián xiāng xī yù	pity the fragrance, cherish the jade: to be tender toward women — 香 (fragrance) and 玉 (jade) both stand for a beautiful woman. To treat women with gentleness and care. Literary, sometimes ironic.
良药苦口	liáng yào kǔ kǒu	good medicine is bitter to the mouth: honest advice is hard to hear — From the Kǒngzǐ Jiāyǔ. Often completed by 忠言逆耳 (loyal words grate on the ear). Used to soften unwelcome but useful criticism.
落花流水	luò huā liú shuǐ	fallen blossoms and flowing water — spring's end; utter defeat — Lit: falling-flowers flowing-water. A classical image from Tang and Song poetry for the beauty of spring's end. Also used for utter defeat: 打得落花流水 "beaten to pieces."
埋头苦干	mái tóu kǔ gàn	to bury the head and toil hard: to work diligently without distraction — Lit: bury-head-bitter-work. To lower one's head and labor away, absorbed in the task and indifferent to praise or notice. The picture is of someone bent over their work, head down.
满面春风	mǎn miàn chūn fēng	a face full of spring breeze: beaming, radiant with joy — Lit: full-face-spring-wind. The whole face glowing like a warm spring day, said of someone visibly pleased, content, or flush with good fortune.
满纸荒唐言，一把辛酸泪。	mǎn zhǐ huāng táng yán , yī bǎ xīn suān lèi .	A whole page of absurd words, a fistful of bitter tears. — From Cao Xueqin's own opening verse, his description of the book and of the grief that went into it. The pairing of seeming absurdity (a tale of stones and dreams) with real suffering captures the novel's double nature as fantasy and as a wound from the author's own ruined life.
面不改色	miàn bù gǎi sè	"face doesn't change color" — unruffled; keeping composure under pressure; poker-faced — Lit: face-not-change-color. The mark of composure under pressure , your face doesn't betray fear, surprise, or discomfort. 他面不改色地接受了批评，继续进行演讲。"He received the criticism without changing expression and continued his speech." Often admired as a sign of courage or self-discipline.
面红耳赤	miàn hóng ěr chì	face red, ears crimson — flushed with anger, embarrassment, or the heat of argument — 面 miàn (face) + 红 hóng (red) + 耳 ěr (ears) + 赤 chì (the deeper crimson). The face and ears flush red from a surge of emotion, most often a heated argument or acute embarrassment. 两个人争得面红耳赤，谁也不让谁。
面面俱到	miàn miàn jù dào	every aspect covered; thorough in all directions — 面 miàn (face; surface; aspect) + 面 + 俱 jù (all; together) + 到 (arrived at). Arrival has reached every face, every surface, every angle of the matter. Used to praise comprehensive coverage , a plan that misses nothing, a speech that addresses every concern, an analysis that leaves no gap.
面有菜色	miàn yǒu cài sè	a face with the color of greens: gaunt and sallow from hunger — Lit: face-has-vegetable-color. 菜色 is the wan, greenish pallor of someone who has eaten nothing but wild greens, with no grain or meat. The phrase paints a face worn down by famine or hardship.
明月几时有？把酒问青天。	míng yu è jǐ shí yǒu ? bǎ jiǔ wèn qīng tiān .	When did the bright moon first appear? Cup in hand, I ask the blue sky. — Opening of Su Shi's 水调歌头 (Prelude to Water Melody), written on the Mid-Autumn Festival while missing his younger brother Su Zhe.
鸟语花香	niǎo yǔ huā xiāng	birds singing, flowers fragrant: a beautiful spring scene — A stock phrase for an idyllic countryside in spring, the birds talking and the blossoms in scent. Warm and common in description.
牛刀小试	niú dāo xiǎo shì	a small test of the ox-cleaver: great talent on a minor task — From the cook Páo Dīng of the Zhuāngzǐ, whose ox-carving blade glides through every joint. A heavy cleaver lightly tried, said when a capable person handles an easy job and shows the skill in reserve.
庖丁解牛	Pāo dīng jiě niú	Cook Ding carves the ox; acting in perfect accord with natural structure — 庖丁 Pāodīng (Cook Ding , 庖 páo = kitchen, cook; 丁 Dīng = a name/title) + 解 jiě (to separate; to cut apart; to resolve; to understand , the character shows a horn + knife + cow) + 牛 niú (ox; cattle). Zhuangzi's most celebrated parable, from the third chapter (养生主 , The Secret of Caring for Life).
萍水相逢	píng shuǐ xiāng féng	to meet by chance: like duckweed drifting together on water — Lit: duckweed-water mutually-meet. Floating duckweed has no fixed place, so the phrase describes strangers who meet briefly by accident and then drift apart again.
千人千面	qiān rén qiān miàn	a thousand people, a thousand faces — each one different — Often used to describe the terracotta warriors, whose faces are all individual. As a general chengyu it means that every person (or every case, or every product) is distinct, with no two alike, and it can praise rich variety or warn against treating different things as the same.
千辛万苦	qiān xīn wàn kǔ	"a thousand pains, ten thousand toils" — untold hardships; every kind of suffering — 千辛 (a thousand bitternesses) and 万苦 (ten thousand toils): hardship piled on hardship beyond counting. Used to describe the immense effort and suffering endured to reach a goal: 历尽千辛万苦 (to have gone through every kind of hardship).
人面兽心	rén miàn shòu xīn	a human face with the heart of a beast: a cruel person behind a civil mask — Not built on 社 itself, but a classic measure of how a person fits or fails the bonds of human society: someone who looks like one of us yet behaves with brute cruelty. A reminder that belonging to a 社会 is judged by conduct, not appearance.
人往高处走，水往低处流	rén wǎng gāo chù zǒu , shuǐ wǎng dī chù liú	"people move toward high places; water flows to low places" — the natural human drive for self-improvement; ambition as gravity — A folk proverb rather than a classical four-character chengyu, but one of the most widely cited sayings about ambition and social mobility in Mandarin. Water follows physical gravity downward; people follow social gravity upward.
仁者乐山，智者乐水	rén zhě yào shān , zhì zhě yào shuǐ	"the benevolent love mountains, the wise love water" — The companion form of the Analects couplet, often quoted with the order reversed. 仁 (benevolence) and 智 (wisdom) are matched to mountain (immobile, supporting) and water (mobile, responsive) respectively.
如鱼得水	rú yú dé shuǐ	like a fish that has found water — to be in one's element; perfectly suited to one's environment — A fish out of water dies; a fish in water is entirely itself. This chengyu describes the state of someone who has found the exact environment where they thrive , a new job that suits them perfectly, a community where they feel at home. 他在新公司如鱼得水，如虎添翼。
杀鸡儆猴	shā jī jǐng hóu	kill the chicken to warn the monkey: punish one to frighten the rest — Make an example of a minor offender so the watching crowd falls into line. Common in talk of office discipline and political crackdowns.
山高水长	shān gāo shuǐ cháng	"mountains high, water long" — virtue or reputation that endures across time — From a Northern Song memorial by Fan Zhongyan (范仲淹) describing the legacy of a virtuous official: 云山苍苍，江水泱泱；先生之风，山高水长 ("the cloudy mountains stretch dark; the rivers run wide; the master's spirit , high as the mountains, long as the rivers").
山清水秀	shān qīng shuǐ xiù	Mountains Clear, Waters Lovely — Clear mountains, lovely waters. The standard Chinese phrase for a picturesque landscape: four characters that compress the whole aesthetic vocabulary of natural beauty.
山穷水尽	shān qióng shuǐ jìn	"the mountains are exhausted, the waters are at their end" — at the end of one's resources — From Lu You (陆游, Song dynasty), 游山西村: 山穷水尽疑无路，柳暗花明又一村 ("when the mountains are spent and the waters are done and there seems to be no road , there, beyond the dim willows and bright flowers, is another village").
山重水复	shān chóng shuǐ fù	mountains piled on mountains, waters winding through waters — a seemingly hopeless impasse — From a poem by Lu You 陆游. Full line: 山重水复疑无路，柳暗花明又一村, "Mountain on mountain, water on water, seemingly no path; then through dark willows and bright flowers, a village appears." The proverb for finding hope when all seems lost.
上善若水	shàng shàn ruò shuǐ	the highest good is like water — effortless giving that sustains without striving — Daodejing, Chapter 8. Functions as a classical four-character literary phrase rather than an everyday idiom. Cited in calligraphy, philosophy, and any context invoking the Daoist ideal of natural, uncontending benefit.
上善若水。	shàng shàn ruò shuǐ .	The highest good is like water. — From chapter 8. Water is the master image of the Daodejing: it benefits all things without contending, it seeks the low places that everyone else disdains, and in this very humility it comes closest to the 道.
书香门第	shū xiāng mén dì	a family with a fragrance of books — a family of scholars and learning — Lit: book-fragrance-door-lineage. A family where books are the tradition and education runs through the generations. High cultural prestige in Chinese society. 书香 shūxiāng "the fragrance of books" is itself a vivid compound.
水到渠成	shuǐ dào qú chéng	when water flows, a channel forms — Literally "when the water arrives, the channel is made", success comes naturally once conditions ripen, with no forcing. It is the gentle counterpart to 终于's hard-won arrival: the long-awaited outcome that, in the end, simply falls into place.
水滴石穿	shuǐ dī shí chuān	water drips and the stone is bored through: small force, long time — The same image reordered, traced to the Han text Hàn Shū. Stresses the result, the worn-through stone, as proof that small efforts accumulate.
水火不容	shuǐ huǒ bù róng	water and fire cannot coexist — irreconcilably opposed — Water extinguishes fire; fire evaporates water. The two simply cannot occupy the same space. Used for people, factions, or ideas in absolute opposition. 他们两人水火不容 "Those two are completely incompatible."
水泄不通	shuǐ xiè bù tōng	"not even water can leak through": packed solid; completely blocked or surrounded — 水 shuǐ (water) + 泄 xiè (to leak; to drain out) + 不 bù (not) + 通 (to pass through). So densely packed that not even a drop of water could trickle out.
水涨船高	shuǐ zhǎng chuán gāo	when the water rises the boat rises too: gains that lift everything along with the tide — Originally a Chán Buddhist phrase. Used when one rising element pulls others up with it, whether prices, status, or a whole industry.
水中捞月	shuǐ zhōng lāo yu è	fishing the moon out of water — chasing an illusion — Lit: water-inside-fish-moon. The moon's reflection in a pond looks real but cannot be grasped. Used for futile efforts chasing something impossible. A Buddhist parable: mistaking the reflection for reality.
顺风顺水	shùn fēng shùn shuǐ	with the wind, with the water — smooth sailing; everything going well without obstruction — A wish and a description: wind at your back, current in your favor , nothing impeding progress. 祝你顺风顺水！(Wishing you smooth sailing!) is a common departure blessing. The 风水 imagery is natural: when wind and water cooperate, travel is easy; when they oppose, nothing moves.

Describes the pattern of letting one's face-consciousness cause unnecessary suffering , not asking for help, not admitting failure, not seeking medical attention, because doing so would cost face.
四面八方	sì miàn bā fāng	"four faces, eight directions" — from every direction; from everywhere — The most common chengyu using 方. 四面 (the four faces of a square; the four sides) + 八方 (the eight directions). Used for crowds, news, demands, attacks , anything converging from every direction. 来自四面八方的客人 (guests from all over).
四面楚歌	sì miàn chǔ gē	"Chu songs on all four sides" — besieged on every side; utterly isolated — From the fall of Xiang Yu, the Hegemon-King of Chu, at the battle of Gaixia (202 BCE). Surrounded by Han forces, Xiang Yu heard his enemies singing the songs of his own homeland of Chu from every direction, and concluded that Chu had already fallen and his men had deserted to the enemy.

同甘共苦	tóng gān gòng kǔ	to share joys and hardships — Literally "together the sweet, shared the bitter", to go through good times and bad side by side. The second half (为时未晚, not yet too late) is the direct answer to the fatalist in the original story. The full eight-character phrase is common enough to function as a single fixed saying.
亡羊得牛	wáng yáng dé niú	lose a sheep, gain an ox — A related phrase (not a classical chengyu in the same sense) used to describe a small loss that leads to an unexpected larger gain: the silver lining formulation. 猪八戒	zhū bā jiè	Zhu Bajie — the pig, comic appetite — A former marshal of Heaven banished into a pig's body for misconduct, ruled by his appetites for food, sleep, and women, lazy, cowardly, and always scheming to abandon the quest. Yet he is also warm, funny, and ultimately faithful.
猪肉	zhū ròu	pork — 猪 (zhū, pig) + 肉 (meat). The most-eaten meat in China, so common that 肉 alone often means pork by default in recipes.
主食	zhǔ shí	Staple food — Staple food: the rice, noodles, or dumplings served at the end of a Chinese meal. The late arrival of the staple signals that the banquet was generous enough that no one needed to fill up on carbohydrates.
煮蛋	zhǔ dàn	boiled egg
子非鱼，安知鱼之乐？	zǐ fēi yú , ān zhī yú zhī lè ?	You are not a fish — how do you know the happiness of fish? — From 秋水 (Autumn Floods), one of the Outer Chapters. Huizi challenges Zhuangzi's claim that the fish are happy. The exchange is a philosophical demonstration of perspectivism: every claim about the inner states of others is made from outside those states.
紫砂壶	zǐ shā hú	purple clay teapot — the Yixing treasure
粽子	zòng zi	rice dumpling wrapped in leaves — Sticky rice dumplings wrapped in bamboo or reed leaves, traditionally triangular or cylindrical. Made with various fillings: red bean paste, salted egg yolk, cured pork. The characteristic food of the Dragon Boat Festival, eaten to commemorate Qu Yuan.
做菜	zuò cài	to cook; to prepare dishes — 做 zuò (to make) + 菜 cài (dish). To cook, in the sense of making the dishes for a meal. 他很会做菜 "he is a good cook." Its near-neighbor 做饭 zuò fàn frames the same act around the staple rice.
做饭	zuò fàn	to cook (a meal)
做青	zuò qīng	working the green — the oxidation control step
