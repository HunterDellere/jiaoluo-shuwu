//角落書屋/Slices/Family & Relationships
产	chǎn	to produce, give birth, property, output — From birth to bounty: a character that spans biological generation and industrial output, linking the miracle of birth to the logic of production and wealth.
房	fáng	building, house, room — A door radical leaning over the phonetic 方: the side-chamber under a roof that grew into every word for house, room, and the family branches that fill them.
夫	fū	husband, a grown man, laborer; the literary particle fú — A grown man pictured with a hairpin marking adulthood: the husband and the married couple (丈夫 夫妻 夫人 夫妇), the adult man and laborer (农夫 渔夫 大夫), skill and effort (功夫 工夫), and the classical literary particle 夫 fú that opens a sentence.
父	fù	father, male elder, household head — A hand gripping a stone axe: the tool of the one who heads the household, drawn as the figure of the father himself.
哥	gē	elder brother, a familiar term for an older young man — Two 可 stacked into one glyph; the doubled phonetic came to carry the warmest word in the family, elder brother.
孩	hái	baby, child, children — A child-radical fused with the earthly branch 亥: 子 carries the meaning, 亥 lends the rising sound hái.
家	jiā	home, family — House, family, school of thought, nation (国家): the fractal of belonging.
姐	jiě	elder sister, miss, young lady — A woman radical fused to the sound-lender 且, 姐 names the elder sister and stretches to cover every miss and young lady in modern address.
老	lǎo	old, venerable, experienced — A stooped elder with long hair: age as wisdom, seniority as moral authority, and the Daoist sage who wrote the Daodejing.
女	nǚ	woman, female — A kneeling figure with crossed arms: one of the most productive radicals in the script, present in characters for family relationships, feelings, and cultural concepts.
朋	péng	friend, companion; of the same group — Two strings of cowrie shells hung side by side, an old unit of value, borrowed early for companions of equal standing who keep close company.
人	rén	person, humanity — Two strokes, barely changed since the oracle bones, write 人 rén; add 二 and you get 仁, Confucian benevolence.
外	wài	outside, outer, foreign, besides — A diviner reading the shells at the wrong hour, off the normal schedule. From that image grows everything beyond the boundary: the outside, the outer surface, the foreign land, and the relatives on the mother's side.
喜	xǐ	joy, to like, happy events — A festive drum sounding above an open mouth: the sound of joy and the smiling mouth that answers it. From this image grow the words for liking, delight, and every happy occasion, above all the wedding.
长	cháng	long; to grow; chief — A person with flowing long hair. One character, two readings: cháng (long) and zhǎng (to grow, elder), the richest dual-reading in Mandarin.
子	zǐ	child, seed, Master — A picture of a swaddled infant that grew into one of the busiest characters in the language: a child and a son, the honorific for a Master, the empty noun-suffix on a thousand words, and the seed of a thing.
春节	Chūn jié	Spring Festival — The most important holiday in the Chinese calendar: customs, foods, taboos, the reunion dinner, and the cultural weight of the Spring Festival.
工夫茶	gōng fu chá	Gongfu Tea — Tea prepared with skill and time: the Southern Chinese brewing method that defines serious tea culture, built on small vessels, multiple short steeps, and total attention.
功夫	gōng fu	effort, skill over time — Mastery earned through time: martial arts is the paradigm case, but the word covers any deeply cultivated skill.
君子	jūn zǐ	the exemplary person — The Confucian moral ideal that replaced aristocratic birth with cultivated virtue as the basis of social authority: anyone who does the work of 仁义礼智信 qualifies.
面子	miàn zi	face, honor — Giving, losing, and saving face: the social grammar of dignity.
朋友	péng you	friend — The everyday word for friend, and the rich web of relationship terms (好朋友, 男朋友, 交朋友) built around it.
人间	rén jiān	the human world; the mortal realm — The world of people: where karma plays out, where flowers fade in April, and where the only beings who can hear the Dharma are born.
人生	rén shēng	human life; one's life journey — Human life: not biological existence, but the arc of a life lived: its meaning, its seasons, its reckoning.
天人合一	tiān rén hé yī	Heaven and humanity as one: the unity of cosmic and human order — The organizing claim of Chinese cosmology: Heaven and humanity are not separate domains, and human flourishing depends on alignment with the natural order.
小人	xiǎo rén	the petty person — Confucius took a word that meant "commoner" and turned it into the permanent opposite of the gentleman: the small-hearted schemer of every office.
孝	xiào	filial piety — An elder supported by a child's hands: the cardinal Confucian virtue that placed care for parents at the foundation of all moral life.
一会儿	yí huì r	a short while — The everyday word for a little while, how it marks both a short duration and the near future, and how it differs from the verb-softener 一下 and from 等会儿.
祖先	zǔ xiān	ancestors; forebears — 祖先 sits at the center of Chinese religious life: ancestor veneration is a living practice through which families maintain active, reciprocal continuity with the dead.
爱美之心，人皆有之	ài měi zhī xīn , rén jiē yǒu zhī	the love of beauty is universal — everyone has a heart for beauty — Classical proverb affirming the universality of aesthetic appreciation. Often used to gently excuse vanity or the appreciation of beautiful people and things.
饱以老拳	bǎo yǐ lǎo quán	to treat to a full helping of fists: to give a sound beating — A grim joke on the eating sense: instead of a meal, the recipient gets a generous serving of punches. From the Jìn Shū (晋书). Vivid and slightly humorous in tone.
宾朋满座	bīn péng mǎn zuò	guests and friends fill the seats: a house full of company — Lit: guests-friends fill the seats. 宾朋 pairs guest with friend; the image is a hall packed with visitors. Close kin to 高朋满座, used of a lively, well-attended occasion where every seat is taken.
伯牙绝弦	Bó Yá ju é xián	Boya breaks his strings — the loss of a 知音 — From the Liezi 列子. After Zhong Ziqi died, Boya destroyed his qin and played no more. 绝弦 juéxián (severing the strings) became the image for grief over the loss of someone who truly understood you.
草菅人命	cǎo jiān rén mìng	to treat human life as cheap as grass: to kill or harm people without regard — From the Hanshu account of Jia Yi. 菅 jiān is a coarse wild grass; to regard a person's life as no more than a weed is to be utterly callous about killing. Used of tyrants and reckless rulers.
恻隐之心，人皆有之。	cè yǐn zhī xīn , rén jiē yǒu zhī .	The heart of compassion — every person has it. — Mencius 6A:6. The full passage lists all four hearts (恻隐、羞恶、恭敬、是非之心) and asserts that all four are universal: "every person has them." This is the foundation of the 性善论 argument: if the moral emotions are universal, they are innate, not learned.
成家立业	chéng jiā lì yè	start a family and establish a career — the two milestones of adult life — 立业 = to establish one's career or livelihood. The traditional sequence of adult life goals: first you build a home; then you build a livelihood. 他终于成家立业了。(He has finally started a family and established himself.) The phrase encodes a whole theory of the proper human life cycle.
城隍爷	Chéng huáng yé	City God (respectful address) — The suffix 爷 yé is an honorific for senior male figures, divine and human: 土地爷, 老天爷. It conveys respectful familiarity rather than formal distance. Worshippers address the City God as 城隍爷 in prayer and casual speech; formal temple documents may use the full title with rank designation.
出人意料	chū rén yì liào	"beyond people's expectation" — unexpected; surprising; contrary to what was anticipated — Lit: exceed-people-intention-anticipate. One of the most common ways to say "surprisingly" or "unexpectedly" in formal Chinese. 这个结果出人意料，没有人预测到。"This result was unexpected , no one had predicted it." Also appears in the variant 出乎意料 chū hū yìliào with identical meaning.
存天理，灭人欲	cún tiān lǐ , miè rén yù	preserve Heavenly Principle, eliminate human desire — Zhu Xi's formulation of moral cultivation — Perhaps the most quoted and most criticized phrase in Neo-Confucianism. 天理 tiānlǐ = Heavenly Principle (the universal moral norm); 人欲 rén yù = human desires (not all desires but those that conflict with Heavenly Principle: excessive appetite, selfishness).
大哥别说二哥	dà gē bié shuō èr gē	the eldest shouldn't mock the second: pot calling the kettle black — A proverb on shared flaws: 大哥 and 二哥 are equally at fault, so neither has standing to criticize the other. The Mandarin take on glass houses.
大姐大	dà jiě dà	top sister: a commanding, dominant woman — A colloquial trigram, not a classical idiom, for the female leader of a group or a forceful, authoritative woman. Carries a half-admiring, half-wary tone.
大快人心	dà kuài rén xīn	greatly satisfying to the people's hearts — a righteous outcome that pleases everyone — Lit: greatly-fast/free-people's-hearts. Used when a wrongdoer receives punishment, a long-overdue verdict is handed down, or a corrupt official is finally removed. The 快 here is squarely in the "heart released from obstruction" sense: justice has cleared away what was blocking everyone.
待人接物	dài rén jiē wù	to treat people and handle affairs: the way one conducts oneself with others — Lit: treat-people-engage-things. The whole manner of dealing with others and managing matters; said in praise of someone whose conduct is gracious and well judged.
丢人现眼	diū rén xiàn yǎn	"lose people, show eyes" — to make a spectacle of oneself; disgraceful public behavior — Lit: lose-person-expose-eyes. A stronger and more colorful version of 丢脸 , specifically about embarrassing public display. 现眼 (to expose one's eyes to public ridicule) adds the dimension of being stared at and judged.
洞房花烛	dòng fáng huā zhú	bridal chamber and wedding candles: the wedding night — 洞房 is the bridal chamber, the deep inner 房 of the house; 花烛 are the red wedding candles. The set phrase stands for a marriage celebration, often paired as 洞房花烛夜, the night of the wedding.
房谋杜断	fáng móu dù duàn	Fáng plans, Dù decides: a perfect pairing of complementary talents — From the Tang statesmen Fáng Xuánlíng, famed for strategy, and Dù Rúhuì, famed for decisiveness. Here 房 is the surname Fáng; the idiom praises two people whose strengths complete each other.
封妻荫子	fēng qī yìn zǐ	titles for the wife, privilege for the sons: rank and honor extended to one's family — The reward of high office under the empire: a man's success earned a title for his wife and protected status for his sons. Evokes the prestige of the old examination-and-rank system.
夫唱妇随	fū chàng fù suí	the husband sings and the wife follows: a harmonious married couple — Literally "the husband leads, the wife accompanies." A traditional image of marital harmony in which the couple act in accord, one taking the lead and the other in step.
父慈子孝	fù cí zǐ xiào	the father kind, the son filial: the reciprocal duty between generations — A Confucian formula for the father-son relationship, paired in the classics with 兄友弟恭 (elder brother caring, younger respectful). Authority and obedience are framed as mutual obligations, not one-way command.
父债子还	fù zhài zǐ huán	the father's debt, the son repays: a debt or wrong passes down the line — Once a literal expectation in family law, now used figuratively for any burden inherited from a previous generation. Carries a tone of grim inevitability.
高朋满座	gāo péng mǎn zuò	distinguished friends fill the seats: a gathering of honored guests — Lit: high-friends fill the seats. 高朋 are eminent guests, the 高 marking rank or worth. The phrase from Wang Bo's preface to the Tengwang Pavilion praises a host whose hall is full of distinguished company, a warm word for a well-attended feast.
哥俩好	gē liǎ hǎo	the two of us are tight: a toast and a drinking-game call — A finger-guessing drinking call and a stock phrase for two close male friends. 俩 (liǎ) is "the two of us," so the whole thing means "we two brothers are solid."
个人主义	gèr én zhǔ yì	individualism — 个人 (the individual) + 主义 (-ism). The modern term for individualism, the doctrine that puts the single person ahead of the collective. Its standing contrast is 集体主义 jítǐ zhǔyì (collectivism).
功夫不负有心人	gōng fu bù fù yǒu xīn rén	effort does not fail the determined person — hard work pays off; persistence wins — One of the most commonly cited Chinese sayings about effort. 有心人 = a person with resolve and intention. The saying frames determination as the prerequisite: effort pays off for those who truly commit to it. 只要坚持，功夫不负有心人。
光宗耀祖	guāng zōng yào zǔ	bring glory to the clan and illuminate the ancestors — 光 (illuminate) + 宗 (clan lineage) + 耀 (cause to shine) + 祖 (ancestors). The foundational aspiration of Chinese family-oriented ambition: achievement is owed to and shared with those who came before. Used sincerely in formal contexts; with ironic distance among younger generations.
狐朋狗友	hú péng gǒu yǒu	fox-friends and dog-companions: a bad crowd — Lit: fox-friends, dog-friends. 狐 fox and 狗 dog stand for the sly and the low. The phrase scorns worthless drinking companions and hangers-on, the kind of friends who lead you nowhere good. A parent's word of warning.
坏人坏事	huài rén huài shì	bad people and bad deeds: wrongdoers and their misdeeds — A set four-character phrase pairing 坏人 with 坏事, often in the formula 与坏人坏事作斗争, "to struggle against wrongdoers and wrongdoing." Carries a formal, almost official register.
皇亲国戚	huáng qīn guó qī	imperial relatives and royal in-laws — The emperor's kin and the families connected to the throne by marriage, the privileged inner circle of the imperial house. Now used, often with a sardonic edge, for anyone who enjoys protection or privilege through family connections to power.
活到老，学到老	huó dào lǎo , xu é dào lǎo	live until old, learn until old — lifelong learning — The most common proverb for lifelong learning: you learn for as long as you live. An encouragement to keep studying at any age, and a gentle reminder that no one has ever learned enough to stop. Equivalent in spirit to the English "you're never too old to learn."
己所不欲，勿施于人	jǐ suǒ bù yù , wù shī yú rén	do not impose on others what you yourself do not want — the Silver Rule — Analects 15.24. Confucius called this 恕 shù (reciprocity, forgiveness), the negative formulation of the Golden Rule. One of the most famous sentences in Chinese. Appears in the UN Declaration of Human Rights as a cross-cultural parallel. 己 = oneself; 施 = to impose/apply; 于人 = onto others.
己所不欲，勿施于人。	jǐ suǒ bù yù , wù shī yú rén .	What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others. — Appears twice in the Analects (12.2, 15.24) as the Confucian formulation of the negative Golden Rule. Called the doctrine of 恕 shù (sympathetic understanding). This formulation was inscribed on the UNESCO building in Paris as one of humanity's foundational ethical principles.
家徒四壁	jiā tú sì bì	home with only four walls — extreme poverty; owning nothing — Lit: home-merely-four-walls. 徒 = only; merely; bare. The household contains nothing but the four walls themselves. Used in literature to describe destitution. Often contrasted with 家财万贯 jiācái wànguàn (immense family wealth). A stock phrase for total financial ruin.
家喻户晓	jiā yù hù xiǎo	known to every household — a household name; universally known — Lit: family-understood-household-known. 喻 = understood; 晓 = known; aware. Every family understands it; every household knows it. 这首歌已经家喻户晓了。(This song is now known to everyone.) The go-to phrase for "universally famous" in Chinese.
江湖骗子	jiāng hú piàn zi	a wandering swindler, a roving con artist — The 江湖 of itinerant healers and fortune-tellers turned into a label for a charlatan who works the crowds.
皆大欢喜	jiē dà huān xǐ	all are greatly delighted — everyone ends up happy — Originally the closing formula of Buddhist sutras, where the assembly rejoices at the teaching and departs; now the phrase for win-win endings, amicable settlements, and comedies. It is also the standard Chinese title of Shakespeare's As You Like It, a translation with better provenance than most.
姐妹情深	jiě mèi qíng shēn	deep sisterly affection — A common modern four-character set phrase for the strong bond between sisters or close female friends. Frequent in titles, toasts, and warm descriptions of female friendship.
借刀杀人	jiè dāo shā rén	borrow a knife to kill a man: destroy a rival by another's hand — The third of the Thirty-Six Stratagems (三十六计). You stay clean while a third party does the deed; still vivid in business and political commentary today.
巾帼英雄	jīn guó yīng xióng	heroine — a woman of exceptional courage and achievement — Lit: kerchief-hood hero. 巾帼 jīnguó is the traditional women's headdress, used by extension to mean women collectively. A 巾帼英雄 is a female hero, a woman who has achieved what is extraordinary even by the standards of men. Hua Mulan 花木兰 is the archetype. Used to praise exceptional women in any field.
金无足赤，人无完人	jīn wú zú chì , rén wú wán rén	no gold is pure, no person is perfect — Pure 24-karat gold is too soft to be durable; impurity is what makes gold useful. The proverb uses this fact to excuse human flaws: perfection is neither achievable nor desirable. Used to express tolerance of others' shortcomings.
尽人事，听天命	jìn rén shì , tīng tiān mìng	do what you can as a human, then accept Heaven's decree — The natural paired response to 事与愿违. Where 事与愿违 names the gap between intention and outcome, this phrase names the correct attitude toward that gap: maximum effort, then acceptance of what lies beyond one's control. The two phrases together describe the full Confucian-Daoist response to adversity.
敬祖尊宗	jìng zǔ zūn zōng	to revere ancestors and honor the lineage — A set phrase expressing the core ethic of ancestor veneration: to show reverence (敬 jìng) to direct ancestors (祖) and to honor the lineage (宗) as a whole. Used in ritual speeches, genealogy prefaces, and New Year ancestral hall ceremonies.
君子不器	jūn zǐ bù qì	the exemplary person is not a vessel — Confucius says the junzi is not a utensil: not defined by function, not limited to one use. The Analects' argument against reducing a person to their usefulness.
君子之交	jūn zǐ zhī jiāo	the friendship of noble people: plain as water, lasting and unselfish — Lit: noble-person's friendship. From the saying 君子之交淡如水, "the friendship of the noble is bland as water," meaning a true bond needs no flattery or gifts and so never sours.
苦口婆心	kǔ kǒu pó xīn	bitter mouth, grandmotherly heart: to urge earnestly and patiently — Describes someone who repeats hard advice tirelessly out of genuine care, the way an old woman nags her grandchild for their own good.
老当益壮	lǎo dāng yì zhuàng	old but growing ever stronger — vigorous in old age — Lit: old-yet-increasingly-vigorous. From the Book of the Later Han: the general Ma Yuan said, "A man of ambition grows more vigorous as he grows old; a hero grows stronger with age." Used to praise an older person who retains energy, capability, and drive. A compliment, never ironic.
老调重弹	lǎo diào chóng tán	to rehash old ideas: to bring out a tired theme again — Lit: to pluck the old tune once more. A dismissive phrase for repeating a stale argument or worn-out idea as if it were new. Here 调 takes the diào reading, "a tune, a melody."
老马识途	lǎo mǎ shí tú	an old horse knows the road — experience guides better than intelligence — From the Han Feizi: Duke Huan of Qi was lost in the mountains; an advisor suggested releasing an old war horse, which led the army home by the familiar road. Used to justify consulting experienced people rather than relying on theory or intelligence alone. One of the most famous 老 idioms.
老少皆宜	lǎo shào jiē yí	"appropriate for old and young alike" — suitable for everyone — 老 (old) + 少 shào (young) + 皆 (all, both) + 宜 (appropriate, fitting). The old-young pair covers the full lifespan: if something suits both extremes, it suits everyone.
老生常谈	lǎo shēng cháng tán	an old scholar's stock talk: a hackneyed, oft-repeated remark — Lit: old-scholar-often-says. A truism so worn from repetition that it carries little weight, the Chinese equivalent of a tired platitude.
里应外合	lǐ yīng wài hé	"inside responds, outside coordinates" — inside-outside cooperation; a coordinated attack from within and without — 里 (inside) + 应 (yīng, to respond, to correspond) + 外 (outside) + 合 (to coordinate, to join). A military and strategic idiom: the force inside a city or position responds to the force attacking from outside, each coordinating with the other.
练达老成	liàn dá lǎo chéng	practiced and worldly, mature and steady — 练达 means seasoned in the ways of the world; pairs with 老成 (mature beyond one's years). A literary compliment for someone whose poise comes from experience, not age alone.
列祖列宗	liè zǔ liè zōng	all the ancestors of the line, generation upon generation — 列 (arrayed, successive) + 祖 + 列 + 宗. A formulaic invocation of the entire ancestral chain, used in formal toasts, funeral eulogies, and solemn declarations where one speaks on behalf of the whole family line. The repetition of 列 creates a sense of unbroken succession stretching backward.
六亲不认	liù qīn bù rèn	to disown all six kinships — utterly cold-blooded, recognizing no family bond — Lit: six-relatives-not-recognize. The classical six kinships are father, mother, elder brother, younger brother, wife, child. Said of someone who in the pursuit of power or self-interest will not even acknowledge their own kin.
妈祖显灵	mā zǔ xiǎn líng	Mazu's divine power manifests — a miracle attributed to her — 显灵 xiǎn líng (lit. "spirit manifests") is the standard phrase for a deity's miraculous intervention. 妈祖显灵 is used when a ship survives a storm, a prayer is answered, or an illness is healed , any event attributed to Mazu's protective intervention.
满街都是圣人	mǎn jiē dōu shì shèng rén	saints fill the streets — everyone already has the innate moral knowledge to be a sage — Said to have been uttered by Wang Yangming after a student asked skeptically whether ordinary people could really possess 良知. His response: walk out onto any busy street and observe; anyone who acts from their innate moral sense in that moment is a sage.
孟姜女哭长城	Mèng Jiāng nü kū Cháng chéng	"Meng Jiangnu weeping at the Great Wall" — grief strong enough to move stone — One of China's four great folk legends (四大民间故事 sì dà mínjiān gùshì). Meng Jiangnu's husband Fan Xiliang was conscripted to build the Qin wall and died of exhaustion.
孟母三迁	Mèng mǔ sān qiān	Mencius's mother moves three times — the importance of environment in education — Not from the Mencius text itself but from the Lienüzhuan 列女传 (Biographies of Exemplary Women), attributed to Liu Xiang 刘向 (77–6 BCE).
目中无人	mù zhōng wú rén	"no people in the eye" — arrogant; treating no one as worthy of notice — 目 (eye) + 中 (within) + 无 (without) + 人 (people). Looking out from one's eye and seeing no one worth registering , arrogance so total that others become invisible. A criticism of conceit and contempt. 目空一切 (eye-empty-everything) intensifies this further: not even things are worth looking at.
内外有别	nèi wài yǒu bié	inside and outside are distinct: insiders and outsiders are treated differently — Lit: inside-outside-have-distinction. The principle that there is a proper line between those within a family, group, or nation and those without, and that the two are not handled the same way.
朋比为奸	péng bǐ wéi jiān	to band together for evil: to collude in wrongdoing — Lit: friends-allying to-do evil. 朋比 is to band together as a clique, 为奸 to commit villainy. A sharply negative idiom for cronies who join forces for corrupt or treacherous ends, the dark side of company kept on a level footing.
朋友遍天下	péng you biàn tiān xià	to have friends all over the world — A warm saying praising someone with a wide circle of friends everywhere. 遍 biàn means "all over," 天下 "under heaven, the whole world." Often paired with the encouraging proverb 在家靠父母，出门靠朋友 ("at home you rely on parents; away from home you rely on friends"), which captures the practical importance of…
匹夫之勇	pǐ fū zhī yǒng	the courage of a common man: reckless bravery without strategy — 匹夫 pǐfū is an ordinary fellow of no rank. The phrase names the brute, headlong courage of one who rushes in to fight without thought or plan, contrasted with the cool, calculated valor of a true leader. It is a gentle put-down: brave, but foolishly so.
平易近人	píng yì jìn rén	"level and easy, close to people" — approachable; unpretentious; accessible — 平易 (flat and easy , without obstacles or pretension) + 近 (close to; approachable) + 人 (people). A person who is 平易近人 does not stand on ceremony, does not use their position as a barrier, and can be approached by ordinary people without formality or anxiety.
千夫所指	qiān fū suǒ zhǐ	pointed at by a thousand men: condemned by everyone — Literally "what a thousand men point their fingers at." It describes a person reviled by the whole public, the target of universal blame and reproach. To be 千夫所指 is to stand utterly condemned in the eyes of the crowd, a heavy figure for public disgrace.
千人千面	qiān rén qiān miàn	a thousand people, a thousand faces — each one different — Often used to describe the terracotta warriors, whose faces are all individual. As a general chengyu it means that every person (or every case, or every product) is distinct, with no two alike, and it can praise rich variety or warn against treating different things as the same.
谦谦君子	qiān qiān jūn zǐ	"the humbly modest exemplary person" — the junzi whose modesty is doubled and genuine — From the 易经 (Yijing), first line of hexagram 15 谦 Qiān: 谦谦君子，用涉大川，吉 (The humbly modest exemplary person , it is advantageous to cross the great river. Good fortune). The doubling of 谦谦 marks the quality as thorough and genuine rather than surface-level.
亲密无间	qīn mì wú jiàn	close with no gap between: utterly intimate, inseparable — Lit: intimate-close-without-gap. Of a friendship or bond so tight that no space, no estrangement, divides the two. Here 间 takes the jiàn reading, the gap as separation.
亲贤臣，远小人	qīn xián chén , yuǎn xiǎo rén	keep worthy ministers close and petty men far — From Zhuge Liang's Chushibiao (出师表), the memorial he submitted before the northern campaigns, diagnosing why the Former Han rose and the Later Han fell.
取长补短	qǔ cháng bǔ duǎn	take strengths to補 compensate weaknesses — learn from others — Lit: take-long-补-short. The standard phrase for learning from others to compensate for one's own deficiencies. 互相取长补短 "learn from each other's strengths to make up for each other's weaknesses" is the common collaborative formulation.
人定胜天	rén dìng shèng tiān	human will can overcome heaven — perseverance defeats fate — The Maoist rejoinder to classical fatalism. 人定 = human resolution and determination; 胜 = to overcome; 天 = heaven/fate. Against the traditional acceptance of 天命, this phrase asserts that collective human effort can reshape nature and destiny.
人尽其才	rén jìn qí cái	every person fully uses their talents; no talent is wasted — Lit: person-exhaust-their-talent. A governance and organizational ideal: the right people in the right roles, fully realizing their abilities. Often paired with 物尽其用 wù jìn qí yòng (every object fully utilized).
人困马乏	rén kùn mǎ fá	men worn out and horses spent: an army exhausted — The classic picture of a column at the end of a long march, fighters and mounts alike drained. Frequent in historical novels such as the Sānguó Yǎnyì.
人面兽心	rén miàn shòu xīn	a human face with the heart of a beast: a cruel person behind a civil mask — Not built on 社 itself, but a classic measure of how a person fits or fails the bonds of human society: someone who looks like one of us yet behaves with brute cruelty. A reminder that belonging to a 社会 is judged by conduct, not appearance.
人命关天	rén mìng guān tiān	a human life is a matter of Heaven; "lives are at stake," used to underscore extreme gravity — 人命 "human life" plus 关天 "concerns Heaven." The phrase invoked when human lives are on the line in medical, legal, or rescue contexts, to communicate that ordinary calculations no longer apply. The implicit logic: a life belongs to Heaven; touching it is a cosmic, not merely human, matter.
人山人海	rén shān rén hǎi	a mountain of people, a sea of people — an enormous crowd — Lit: person-mountain-person-sea. The go-to phrase for describing massive crowds: national holidays, concerts, train stations during Spring Festival rush. Pairing 山 (mountain) with 海 (sea), the two largest natural forms, captures the sense of scale without irony.
人神相通	rén shén xiāng tōng	humans and spirits communing — the fundamental premise of shamanism — Lit: human-spirit-mutually-communicate. The cosmological axiom that makes shamanism possible: the boundary between the human world and the spirit world is permeable, and certain human specialists can cross it.
人算不如天算	rén suàn bù rú tiān suàn	human plans yield to Heaven's plans — fate overrides strategy — Lit: human calculations are no match for Heaven's calculations. A fatalist proverb at the heart of folk cosmology: 谋事在人，成事在天 (planning is human, success is Heaven's). Used when the best-laid plans go awry.
人往高处走，水往低处流	rén wǎng gāo chù zǒu , shuǐ wǎng dī chù liú	"people move toward high places; water flows to low places" — the natural human drive for self-improvement; ambition as gravity — A folk proverb rather than a classical four-character chengyu, but one of the most widely cited sayings about ambition and social mobility in Mandarin. Water follows physical gravity downward; people follow social gravity upward.
人为财死，鸟为食亡	rén wèi cái sǐ , niǎo wèi shí wáng	people die for money; birds die for food — 财 cái = wealth, property. One of the most widely quoted Chinese proverbs about greed and human nature, a parallel structure comparing human ambition to animal survival instinct. Sobering, not condemnatory; an observation about the force of desire.
人心向背	rén xīn xiàng bèi	whether hearts face toward or turn away: popular support or opposition — Lit: people's-hearts, facing-or-backing. 向 is to face toward (support), 背 to turn one's back (oppose). The phrase names the balance of public sentiment that decides whether a cause or a ruler will stand.
人言可畏	rén yán kě wèi	what people say is to be feared — A shorter, more colloquial formulation of the same truth: public opinion is a force that should not be underestimated. Less analytical than 三人成虎, more emotionally immediate. Often used in the context of reputation and social pressure.
人在曹营心在汉	rén zài Cáo yíng xīn zài Hàn	body in Cao's camp, heart in Han — physically present but mentally elsewhere; loyalty divided — From Romance of the Three Kingdoms: Guan Yu was held in Cao Cao's camp but his heart remained loyal to Liu Bei's Han cause. The 在…在 parallel structure contrasts physical location (body) with emotional location (heart).
人在江湖身不由己	rén zài jiāng hú shēn bù yóu jǐ	in the rivers-and-lakes world, one's body is not one's own — once entered, you are bound by its rules — The defining 江湖 saying. Used seriously to explain why one cannot simply walk away from obligations , and humorously to explain why you have to attend yet another obligatory dinner, meeting, or social engagement.
仁者爱人	rén zhě ài rén	the benevolent person loves others — the most concise definition of 仁 — From Mencius (4B:28). The simplest, most direct statement of the first virtue. 仁 is not an abstract principle but an orientation toward actual people. Love here (爱 ài) means caring about the genuine wellbeing of others, not sentimental attachment.
认贼作父	rèn zéi zuò fù	to take a thief for a father: to serve one's enemy as a benefactor — A harsh condemnation, aimed at a traitor or collaborator who submits to the very people who wronged him. The image inverts the sacred father-son bond into betrayal.
认祖归宗	rèn zǔ guī zōng	to acknowledge one's ancestors and return to the lineage — Lit: recognize-ancestor-return-lineage. Used for those who were separated from their family (by adoption, diaspora, or historical disruption) and formally reconnect with their ancestral line. A profoundly significant act in Chinese social life.
任人宰割	rèn rén zǎi gē	to be at the mercy of others; to be slaughtered at will by whoever wants to — Lit: let-people-slaughter-carve. The extreme end of the 被 spectrum , not just being acted upon but being vulnerable to anyone who chooses to exploit you. 宰割 = to slaughter and carve up (animals, and by extension, the weak).
三人成虎	sān rén chéng hǔ	three people make a tiger — A minister warns his king: if three people all claim there is a tiger in the marketplace, even the skeptical will believe it. The Zhanguo Ce parable of rumor that outlasts the warning against it.
三人行，必有我师	sān rén xíng , bì yǒu wǒ shī	where three walk together, one of them is sure to be my teacher — From the Analects (论语). Confucius's most-quoted statement on learning: in any company of three people, there will be something to learn , from the better among them by emulation, from the worse by avoidance.
舍己为人	shě jǐ wèi rén	to sacrifice oneself for others: selfless devotion to others' good — Lit: give-up-self-for-others. The highest praise for selflessness, setting one's own interests aside for the sake of other people. The 己 here is exactly the self that 舍 (to give up) renounces.
慎终追远	shèn zhōng zhuī yuǎn	careful attention to death rites and honoring distant ancestors — From the Analects (论语): 慎终追远，民德归厚矣. "When the people are careful about death rites and diligently honor distant ancestors, the virtue of the people will return to richness." Confucius's most concise statement of why ancestor veneration matters: it cultivates moral seriousness in the living.
十年树木，百年树人	shí nián shù mù , bǎi nián shù rén	"ten years to grow a tree, a hundred years to cultivate a person" — education takes far longer than any other cultivation — From the classical text 管子 Guǎnzi. Growing a tree requires ten years of patience: planting, watering, waiting. Cultivating a human being , educating, civilizing, forming character , requires ten times longer. The century-scale horizon is a deliberate rebuke to impatience.
事在人为	shì zài rén wéi	things depend on human effort — nothing is impossible with effort — Lit: matters-located-in-human-doing. The outcome of things lies in human action, not fate or circumstance. An optimistic proverb of agency: no matter how difficult, the result is in human hands. 不要灰心，事在人为。
受制于人	shòu zhì yú rén	to be controlled by others; to be subject to another's power — Lit: receive-control-by-people. The standard formal expression for being under another's power, being constrained by external forces rather than acting freely. Common in diplomatic, strategic, and business contexts. The grammatical structure 受…于 is classical , modern equivalent would be 被人控制.
数典忘祖	shǔ diǎn wàng zǔ	recite the classics but forget your ancestors — From the Zuozhuan: a minister who could cite all historical precedents of his state but could not name his own ancestral lineage was mocked with this phrase. Used for ingratitude, forgetting one's origins, or adopting foreign ways at the expense of one's own heritage.
顺天应人	shùn tiān yìng rén	"accord with Heaven, respond to humanity" — acting in alignment with both cosmic order and popular sentiment — Lit: accord-Heaven-respond-humanity. A classical formula used to describe righteous political action or revolution: the actor is both aligned with Heaven's pattern and responding to the genuine needs of the people.
死而复生，再世为人	sǐ ér fù shēng , zài shì wéi rén	to die and come back to life; to be reborn into the world — 再世 (again-in-the-world) is the classical expression for reincarnation or miraculous revival. Used both literally (near-death recovery) and figuratively (total transformation of a situation or person). 再世英雄 (a hero reborn in a new era).
死要面子活受罪	sǐ yào miàn zi huó shòu zuì	"dying for face, living in torment" — to suffer rather than ask for help because of pride — A seven-character folk saying rather than a classical 成语, but extremely widely used. Describes the pattern of letting one's face-consciousness cause unnecessary suffering , not asking for help, not admitting failure, not seeking medical attention, because doing so would cost face.
四海为家	sì hǎi wéi jiā	to make one's home in all four seas — at home anywhere; cosmopolitan; rootless wandering — 四海 = the four seas = everywhere under heaven. 为 = to treat as; to make into. The whole globe is one's household. Used for travelers, exiles, and those rootlessly at ease anywhere. Both admiring (cosmopolitan freedom) and elegiac (loss of fixed roots) , depending on context.
岁月不饶人	suì yu è bù ráo rén	the years spare no one — time is merciless; age catches up with everyone — 岁月 suìyuè (years and months , a classical compound for the flow of time) + 不饶 bù ráo (shows no mercy, does not spare). A cousin-phrase to 时不我待, but the mood is different: less urgent, more resigned. Where 时不我待 urges action, 岁月不饶人 acknowledges what cannot be undone.
所谓伊人	suǒ wèi yī rén	"the one of whom I speak" — the beloved, the one I yearn for — From the Shijing (诗经) poem 蒹葭 ("Reeds"). One of the most famous lines of classical poetry, and the locus classicus for 伊 as feminine third person. The poem itself is gender-ambiguous in the classical text but read across two millennia as referring to a beloved woman across the river.
桃李满天下	táo lǐ mǎn tiān xià	peaches and plums fill the world: a teacher's students are everywhere — Lit: peach-plum-fill-the-world. 桃李 (peach and plum trees) stand for a teacher's 弟子, the disciples cultivated over a lifetime. To say someone's 桃李满天下 is high praise for a great educator.
天后圣母	Tiān hòu Shèng mǔ	Heavenly Empress, Holy Mother — Mazu's full honorific — The full formal title combines 天后 (Heavenly Empress, the highest imperial designation) with 圣母 Shèngmǔ (Holy Mother, a term shared with the Blessed Virgin in Catholic contexts, creating interesting resonance in Fujian where Portuguese missionaries arrived in the 16th century).
天人感应	tiān rén gǎn yìng	Heaven and humanity in sympathetic resonance — cosmic and human realms in constant responsive dialogue — 感 gǎn = to feel, to resonate; 应 yìng = to respond. The Han Confucian doctrine , systematized by 董仲舒 Dǒng Zhòngshū , that Heaven responds to human affairs and humans respond to Heaven's movements, all mediated through the five phases and Yin-Yang cycles.
天人一体	tiān rén yī tǐ	"Heaven and humanity as one body" — the complete unity of cosmic and human orders — A variant formulation of 天人合一, with 体 (body; substance) replacing 合一 (united as one). 一体 emphasizes organic embodied unity , Heaven and humanity are not merely in accord but constitute a single body, a single substance.
天时地利人和	tiān shí dì lì rén hé	"Heaven's timing, Earth's advantages, human harmony" — the three conditions for success — From Mencius (2A.1): the convergence of the right moment from Heaven, favorable terrain from Earth, and human solidarity. A practical expression of 天人合一 in strategic thought: success requires alignment with all three orders simultaneously.
天下无难事只怕有心人	tiān xià wú nán shì , zhǐ pà yǒu xīn rén	under heaven there are no difficult things — only fear the determined person — Determination overcomes all obstacles , there is nothing under heaven so hard that a truly committed person cannot accomplish it. A companion to 功夫不负有心人. 有心人 yǒuxīn rén = a person of resolve and intention. Used as encouragement in the face of daunting tasks: 天下无难事，只怕有心人，你一定能做到。
天下兴亡，匹夫有责	tiān xià xīng wáng , pǐ fū yǒu zé	the rise and fall of the realm is the responsibility of every common person — Attributed to the late Ming / early Qing Confucian scholar 顾炎武 Gù Yánwǔ (1613–1682), though the current form was popularized by Liang Qichao in the late Qing. A founding text of Chinese civic patriotism.
天之骄子	tiān zhī jiāo zǐ	a favored child of Heaven: one singularly blessed by fortune — Lit: Heaven's-spoiled-child. Originally said of the Xiongnu nomads who boasted Heaven's favor; now used of anyone who seems destined for success, sometimes with a touch of envy.
铁哥们儿	tiě gē men r	iron buddies: rock-solid male friends — 铁 (iron) intensifies 哥们儿. The metaphor is metal: a friendship that won't bend or rust. Reserved for the few friends you would drop everything for.
望女成凤	wàng nǚ chéng fèng	hoping your daughter will become a phoenix — parental ambition for daughters — The female counterpart to 望子成龙 wàng zǐ chéng lóng (hoping your son will become a dragon). 凤 fèng (phoenix) is the female auspicious creature paired with 龙 (dragon). The phoenix (凤凰 fènghuáng) represents feminine grace, beauty, and virtue.
望子成龙	wàng zǐ chéng lóng	to hope ones son becomes a dragon: to long for ones child to succeed greatly — Literally "to hope the son becomes a dragon," the dragon being the emblem of supreme success and distinction. The classic expression of parental ambition: the deep hope that ones child will rise high in life. A close partner phrase, 望女成凤, hopes the daughter becomes a phoenix.
为人师表	wéi rén shī biǎo	to be a teacher and model for others: to set an example worthy of imitation — 为人 wéi rén = to act as, to be for others. 师 shī = teacher. 表 biǎo = model; the standard others measure themselves against. To serve as a moral and intellectual example, especially said of teachers who must embody what they teach.
文房四宝	wén fáng sì bǎo	the four treasures of the study: brush, ink, paper, inkstone — 文房 is the scholar's study, the writing 房; 四宝 are its four treasures: 笔 (brush), 墨 (ink), 纸 (paper), 砚 (inkstone). The phrase names the classical toolkit of Chinese calligraphy and painting.
物是人非	wù shì rén fēi	things remain but people have changed — the bittersweet feeling of returning to a place where everything looks the same but everyone is gone — Lit: things-are-people-not. A key phrase in classical Chinese poetry for the emotional experience of historical rupture: looking at unchanged landscapes after dynasties have fallen. Deeply appropriate to the Yuan-Ming transition, when the same cities stood but an entire ruling order had vanished.
喜出望外	xǐ chū wàng wài	joy beyond what one hoped — overjoyed by surprise — 喜 (delight) arriving from outside (外) the range of expectation (望). The precise word for good news you did not dare hope for: the scholarship, the recovered wallet, the friend at the airport gate. Stronger and more specific than 很高兴.
喜气洋洋	xǐ qì yáng yáng	brimming with joy: radiant with a festive, happy spirit — Lit: festive air spilling over. 洋洋 paints joy overflowing in every direction. Said of a face, a household, or a whole town lit up with celebration, a stock phrase at weddings and the New Year.
喜闻乐见	xǐ wén lè jiàn	glad to hear and pleased to see — popular and well-liked — Describes something the public genuinely enjoys and welcomes, literally "glad to hear it and happy to see it." 喜 (glad) and 乐 lè (happy) pair with 闻 (hear) and 见 (see). Often used of art, entertainment, or activities that are widely and warmly received.
先小人后君子	xiān xiǎo rén hòu jūn zǐ	petty men first, gentlemen after — settle terms up front — The contract-signing proverb: state the money, the split, and the deadlines with un-gentlemanly explicitness now, so that everyone can afford to be a gentleman later. Spoken at the start of partnerships between friends precisely because friendship is what it protects.
相亲相爱	xiāng qīn xiāng ài	to be close and loving — mutual affection between people — Lit: mutually-close mutually-love. The standard phrase for loving harmony in a family or couple. Often used in toasts and wedding speeches.
香火	xiāng huo	incense smoke; the vitality of a temple's active veneration — Literally "incense fire" or "incense smoke," but 香火 carries meanings beyond the physical act of burning. A temple with 香火鼎盛 xiānghuo dǐngshèng (incense burning vigorously) is a temple with a healthy, active congregation, good reputation, and real spiritual efficacy.
小孩没娘，说来话长	xiǎo hái méi niáng , shuō lái huà cháng	the child has no mother: it's a long story — A 歇后语 (xiēhòuyǔ) two-part saying. The literal image, a motherless child, sets up the punchline 说来话长, "it's a long story." Spoken as a preface when the backstory is tangled and the speaker hardly knows where to begin.
小孩子家	xiǎo hái zi jiā	just a kid, only a child — Colloquial set phrase. The trailing 家 marks a category of person; said dismissively or fondly of someone too young to know better. "你一个小孩子家懂什么。"
小孩子气	xiǎo hái zi qì	childish, immature in manner — Describes adult behavior that is petty or babyish. The 气 (qì) suffix turns a noun into a temperament. Everyday spoken register: "他这样太小孩子气了。"
小人得志	xiǎo rén dé zhì	the petty man gets his moment — power in unworthy hands — 得志 is achieving one's ambitions; attached to 小人 it names the special insufferability of the schemer promoted. The stock description of the new supervisor swaggering through a department, and of every court favorite in the historical novels.
挟天子以令诸侯	xié tiān zǐ yǐ lìng zhū hóu	hold the emperor to command the lords — rule through a puppet figurehead — Cao Cao's signature strategy: control a powerless legitimate ruler and issue your own orders in their name. Now a standard idiom for anyone who wields real power from behind a respectable figurehead, in politics or business. It names the gap between formal authority and actual control.
兄弟姐妹	xiōng dì jiě mèi	brothers and sisters; siblings — The full set of siblings named in birth-order pairs: 兄 (elder brother), 弟 (younger brother), 姐 (elder sister), 妹 (younger sister). A staple four-character phrase, and one of the few common ones to carry 姐 directly.
休戚相关	xiū qī xiāng guān	sharing joys and sorrows: bound together by a common fate — Lit: joy-sorrow-mutually-connected. Two parties so closely tied that whatever befalls one befalls the other, the deep 关系 in which fortunes rise and fall as one.
一夫当关	yī fū dāng guān	one man guards the pass: a position so strong that a single defender holds off an army — From the line about the road to Shu, 一夫当关，万夫莫开, "one man holding the pass, ten thousand cannot force it open." It praises a place of such natural strength that a lone defender can block a whole host, and by extension any well-held strategic point or a person stubbornly guarding a position.
一时半会儿	yì shí bàn huì r	in a short time; for a little while — A colloquial set phrase, "one moment, half a spell," meaning a brief span of time, most often in the negative: 这事一时半会儿说不清 ("this can't be explained in a moment"). It builds directly on the 会儿 of 一会儿, stretching it into a fixed expression for "any time soon."
以德服人	yǐ dé fú rén	to win people over by virtue (rather than by force) — Lit: with-virtue-make-people-submit. The Confucian alternative to coercion: a leader whose 德 is genuine attracts willing followers; one who relies on force only gets compliance. The classical contrast is 以力服人 "to make people submit by force" , which produces obedience without loyalty.
以人为本	yǐ rén wéi běn	to put people first; human-centered governance — Lit: use-person-as-root. A classical Confucian governance principle revived as modern policy language. 本 běn (root; foundation) is the underground portion of a tree, the part everything else rests on. The person, not the state or ideology, is the root of all legitimate policy.
以文会友	yǐ wén huì yǒu	"to make friends through writing" — cultural exchange as the basis of friendship — 以 (by means of) + 文 (writing, culture) + 会 (to meet, to gather) + 友 (friend). From the Analects (Book XII): Zēngzǐ says the exemplary person uses 文 (cultural cultivation) to gather friends. In classical usage, 文 here means poetry, ritual texts, and the cultivated arts.
以小人之心，度君子之腹	yǐ xiǎo rén zhī xīn , duó jūn zǐ zhī fù	measuring a gentleman's belly with a petty man's heart — projecting one's own baseness — Adapted from a Zuozhuan phrase: assuming others' motives are as small as one's own. The polite deployment is self-accusation: 是我以小人之心度君子之腹了, "I misjudged you by my own petty standard," the graceful way to withdraw a suspicion that proved unfair.
倚老卖老	yǐ lǎo mài lǎo	leaning on age to trade on age — exploiting seniority — Lit: lean-on-old-sell-old. The critical idiom for elders who demand deference they have not earned, throwing their age around as a trump card to win arguments or avoid accountability.
远亲不如近邻	yuǎn qīn bù rú jìn lín	"a distant relative is no match for a close neighbor" — proximity creates community — 远亲 (distant kin) + 不如 (not as good as) + 近邻 (near neighbor). A proverb rather than a classical chengyu with a single attributed source, but documented in Song dynasty collections and widely cited since.
长此以往	cháng cǐ yǐ wǎng	if this goes on for long — a warning about sustained bad behavior — Lit: long-this-by-going. Used to warn that if a current (usually bad) situation continues indefinitely, serious consequences will follow. 长此以往，身体会垮掉的 "If this keeps up, your health will collapse." Almost always negative; rarely used for positive sustained trends.
长风破浪	cháng fēng pò làng	a long wind breaking through the waves — bold ambition and perseverance — From the Tang poet Li Bai: 长风破浪会有时，直挂云帆济沧海. "There will come a time when the long wind breaks through the waves; I shall set my cloud-sail straight across the vast sea." The image is unstoppable ambition riding the great winds, no obstacle too large to overcome.
长生不老	cháng shēng bù lǎo	long life without ageing — immortality — The obsession that drove the First Emperor, who sent expeditions in search of the elixir of immortality and is said to have died from mercury pills meant to grant it. The chengyu names the dream of eternal life free from ageing, a recurring theme in Daoist alchemy and in the legends around 秦始皇.
真命天子	zhēn mìng tiān zǐ	the true Son of Heaven, destined to rule — The legitimate ruler chosen by Heaven, as opposed to a usurper or pretender. Used in historical fiction and now, loosely and often jokingly, for "the one true destined person" in any field. It rests on the idea that the emperor's authority comes from the 天命 (Mandate of Heaven), not from force alone.
政通人和	zhèng tōng rén hé	government flowing smoothly, people in harmony — the ideal of good governance — From Fan Zhongyan's (范仲淹) "Yueyang Tower Preface" (岳阳楼记, 1046 CE): 政通人和，百废具兴 , when governance flows and the people are in harmony, all that was ruined can be restored. The four-character condensation became a standard formula for describing a well-governed state.
知人者智，自知者明。	zhī rén zhě zhì , zì zhī zhě míng .	To know others is wisdom; to know oneself is enlightenment. — From chapter 33. The Daodejing consistently ranks inward mastery above outward conquest: knowing others is mere cleverness, but knowing oneself is true clarity (明 míng, the brightness of genuine insight); overcoming others takes force, but overcoming oneself takes real strength.
置身事外	zhì shēn shì wài	to place oneself outside the matter: to stay aloof, keep clear of it — Lit: place-body-affair-outside. To keep oneself out of an affair, neither helping nor interfering. Often mildly critical, of someone who stands apart when they ought to be involved.
助人为乐	zhù rén wéi lè	to take joy in helping others — Literally "to help others as one's joy," to find genuine pleasure in aiding people. A prized social virtue, often used to praise the selflessly helpful. Built on 助 (to aid), the same character at the heart of 帮助.
追本溯源	zhuī běn sù yuán	trace back to the root and seek the source — 追 (pursue) + 本 (root) + 溯 (go upstream) + 源 (source). Used for rigorous investigation of origins: of a word, a practice, a family line, a historical claim. The metaphor of going upstream to find the source of a river runs through classical Chinese thought about genealogy and textual scholarship.
子承父业	zǐ chéng fù yè	the son carries on the father's trade: inheriting the family calling — Said when a child takes up the parent's profession or business, from a craftsman's shop to a family firm. Neutral to approving, depending on whether the inheritance is seen as continuity or constraint.
子非鱼，安知鱼之乐	zǐ fēi yú , ān zhī yú zhī lè	You are not a fish — how can you know the happiness of fish? — From the 秋水 chapter. Used in modern Chinese as a rhetorical challenge when someone claims to understand an experience or perspective that is not their own: "You haven't been through what I've been through; how can you know how I feel?" 欢喜	huān xǐ	happy; joyful; delighted — 欢 huān (merry) + 喜 xǐ (glad). A warm, somewhat colloquial word for being happy and pleased, common in the south and in the phrase 欢欢喜喜 "all merry and glad," and in 皆大欢喜 "everyone happy."
黄老之道	Huáng Lǎo Zhī Dào	The Way of Huang-Lao
黄老之学	Huáng Lǎo zhī xu é	Huang-Lao school; the syncretic Daoist-Legalist school that governed early Han China — 黄 Huáng (Yellow Emperor 黄帝, the legendary sage-ruler of antiquity) + 老 Lǎo (Laozi 老子) + 之学 (the learning of).
回家	huí jiā	to go home; to return home — 回 huí (to return) + 家 jiā (home). One of the most common V+location phrases in the language. The directional verb 回 indicates movement back toward an origin point: home as the natural destination of return, not merely a place you happen to go.
婚礼	hūn lǐ	wedding ceremony — 婚 hūn (marriage) + 礼 lǐ (rite; ceremony). Wedding as ritual. The 礼 suffix creates ceremony words for life's great transitions: 婚礼 wedding · 葬礼 zànglǐ funeral · 成人礼 chéngrénlǐ coming-of-age ceremony · 开幕礼 kāimùlǐ opening ceremony.
婚姻	hūn yīn	marriage; matrimony — 婚 hūn (marriage, 女 woman + 昏 dusk, from the tradition of evening weddings) + 姻 yīn (marriage-alliance; matrimony, 女 woman + 因 cause/connection). The formal compound for marriage. 婚礼 hūnlǐ = wedding ceremony. 结婚 jiéhūn = to get married. 离婚 líhūn = to divorce.
活儿	huó r	work; a job; a task; a piece of work — 活 huó (work) + the retroflex 儿 (ér) ending, typical of northern colloquial speech. The concrete job itself: the work to be done, often skilled handwork. A fine piece of craftsmanship is 好活儿 (good work).
急性子	jí xìng zi	an impatient person, hot-tempered type — 急 (impatient) + 性子 (xìngzi, temperament). Someone whose nature is to rush, who cannot stand waiting. The opposite is 慢性子 (mànxìngzi), an unhurried, easygoing type.
忌日	jì rì	death anniversary (ritual day)
祭祖	jì zǔ	ancestral offerings — the core ritual — 祭祖 (lit. "offering to ancestors") is the ritual presentation of food, incense, and goods to deceased family members. The offering table holds cooked dishes (often the ancestor's favorite foods in life), rice, fruit, and wine. Incense is lit and prostrations (拜 bài) are made.
佳节	jiā jié	auspicious festival; festive holiday — literary register — 佳 jiā (excellent; fine; auspicious) + 节 jié (festival; season; node). A slightly elevated term for a holiday or festival occasion, preferred in literary and formal contexts over the more neutral 节日 (jiérì).
家常	jiā cháng	everyday home life; homestyle; ordinary — 家 jiā (home) + 常 cháng (ordinary; often; constant). The routine of domestic life: meals, small talk, everyday rhythms. A warmly neutral word that positions the homely and ordinary as valuable rather than inferior.
家风	jiā fēng	family tradition; values passed through generations — 家 jiā (family) + 风 fēng (wind; custom; style; ethos). The invisible ethos that blows through a family across generations: the unspoken values, behavioral norms, and character traits transmitted from parents to children.
家规	jiā guī	family rules; household rules — 家 jiā (family) + 规 guī (rules; regulations; norms). The explicit code of conduct of a household, the spoken version of what 家风 transmits implicitly. Distinguished families in imperial China often had written 家规 codifying expectations for children's conduct, study, and marriage.
家祭	jiā jì	domestic ancestral offering
家谱	jiā pǔ	genealogy book
家人	jiā rén	family members; one's family — 家 jiā + 人 rén (person/people). The people of the home. More intimate and emotionally warm than the formal compound 家庭成员 (family members, bureaucratic). 家人 feels like "my people": those who share your roof and your life.
家庭	jiā tíng	Family; household — Family; household. The nuclear unit of parents and children who share a home. 家庭作业 (jiātíng zuòyè, homework , lit. "family work") and 家庭医生 (jiātíng yīshēng, family doctor) show the compound in everyday use. The term carries a warmth and domesticity that the more formal 家族 lacks.
家乡	jiā xiāng	hometown; native place — 家 jiā + 乡 xiāng (countryside; native place, the rural origin point). The place where 家 was first made. A place of origin rather than merely an address: family roots, ancestral connections, formative landscape. Central to Chinese identity, deeply tied to belonging and longing.
家训	jiā xùn	family precepts; ancestral rules
家长	jiā zhǎng	head of household; guardian
家族	jiā zú	Clan; extended family network; lineage group sharing a surname. 家族企业 (jiāzú qǐyè) is a family business , often meaning a business controlled across generations by a single clan. The word implies shared ancestry, shared surname, and historically shared clan property and ancestral hall (祠堂 cítáng).
甲子	jiǎ zǐ	the 60-year cycle; first combination
嫁妆	jià zhuang	Dowry , the goods the bride's family sends with her to the new home. Traditionally included furniture, bedding, household items, and valuable objects. The dowry and betrothal gifts are understood as a two-way transfer that balances the exchange between families.
江湖人	jiāng hú rén	a person of the rivers-and-lakes world; someone who lives by the code — The person defined by their membership in the 江湖 , not just someone who passes through it, but someone whose identity is constituted by it. 江湖人 abide by a parallel moral code: loyalty to sworn brothers (义气 yìqi), repayment of debts and favors, the obligation to right wrongs.
江山代有才人出	jiāng shān dài yǒu cái rén chū	every generation of rivers and mountains produces its talented people; each era brings forth its own greatness — From Zhao Yi's (赵翼) 18th-century poem 论诗 (On Poetry): 江山代有才人出，各领风骚数百年 (every generation of rivers and mountains produces its talented people; each holds sway for hundreds of years).
交朋友	jiāo péng you	to make friends — A verb-object phrase: 交 jiāo (to associate with, to form bonds) + 朋友. To make a single friend is 交个朋友 (with 个 splitting the phrase). The same 交 appears in 交流 (to exchange, communicate) and 社交 (social interaction), all built on the sense of mutual exchange.
饺子	jiǎo zi	dumplings — the northern New Year food — The quintessential northern Chinese New Year food, eaten at midnight on New Year's Eve in many northern households. The shape resembles the 元宝 yuánbǎo (ancient sycee gold ingots), making them symbols of wealth.
饺子皮	jiǎo zi pí	Dumpling wrapper, literally "dumpling skin — Dumpling wrapper, literally "dumpling skin." Sold pre-made in supermarkets, but making them by hand is considered superior.
接人	jiē rén	to pick someone up, to meet someone — 接 (to meet) + 人 (rén, person). To go to a station, airport, or door and collect a person who is arriving. The everyday counterpart to ceremonial 迎接.
姐夫	jiě fu	elder sister's husband; brother-in-law — 姐 (elder sister) plus 夫 (husband) names the man married to one's older sister. Chinese kinship marks the relation precisely, distinct from 妹夫 (mèifu), a younger sister's husband.
姐姐	jiě jie	elder sister — The doubled character is the everyday word for one's older sister. The second 姐 takes a neutral tone, giving the warm, soft cadence Chinese kinship terms favor.
姐妹	jiě mèi	sisters; female friends — 姐 (elder sister) plus 妹 (younger sister) yokes the two into a single word for "sisters" as a pair or group, and by extension a tight circle of close female friends.
尽人事听天命	jìn rén shì tīng tiān mìng	do what you can as a human, then accept Heaven's decree
经验	jīng yàn	experience (accumulated know-how) — 经 jīng (pass through) + 验 yàn (to test; verify). The practical know-how gathered by having passed through and tested much: 工作经验 "work experience." Distinct from 经历, which is the events themselves rather than the lessons drawn from them.
惊喜	jīng xǐ	a pleasant surprise; to be pleasantly surprised — 惊 jīng (startled) + 喜 xǐ (joy). The happy jolt of an unexpected good thing: 一个惊喜 "a surprise (gift or event)," 又惊又喜 "both startled and delighted."
镜子	jìng zi	mirror
九九	jiǔ jiǔ	double nine — colloquial shorthand; homophonic longevity wish — 九 jiǔ (nine) + 九 jiǔ (nine). The colloquial compressed name for the festival, and a sound-based wish: 九九 is homophonous with 久久 (jiǔ jiǔ, for a long time; lasting), turning the calendar date into an expression of the wish for long life.
救人	jiù rén	to save / rescue someone — 救 (rescue) + 人 (rén, person). The general act of pulling a person out of danger, whether from water, fire, illness, or ruin.
菊花	jú huā	chrysanthemum — the flower of the ninth month — 菊 jú (chrysanthemum) + 花 huā (flower; blossom). The chrysanthemum blooms in the ninth and tenth lunar months, when most flowering plants have finished. This timing made it a literary symbol of resilience, late-life achievement, and the refusal to conform to expected seasonal rhythms.
巨子	jù zǐ	Grand Master — the head of the Mohist organization — 巨 jù (great; chief) + 子 zǐ (master). The elected or designated leader of the Mohist order, holding authority over all members. The 巨子 could direct members to travel, take on defense contracts for states, and enforce the school's internal law.
句子	jù zi	sentence — 句 jù (sentence) + 子 zi (noun-suffix). The everyday word for a complete sentence as a grammatical unit: 一个句子 "one sentence," 简单的句子 "a simple sentence." The building block every grammar lesson works with.
君君，臣臣，父父，子子	jūn jūn , chén chén , fù fù , zǐ zǐ	let the ruler rule, minister minister, father father, son son — The Analects 12.11 formula for 正名 in practice, given when Duke Jing of Qi asked Confucius about governance. Each binome repeats the same character twice: the first instance is the title, the second is the standard the title entails.
君子协议	jūn zǐ xié yì	gentleman's agreement — A binding agreement resting on personal honor and the parties' characters, without legal documentation. The term assumes both sides are junzi , people whose word is their commitment. Widely used in modern business and political contexts where formal contracts are either inappropriate or impossible.
坎儿井	kǎn er jǐng	Karez — Karez: the ancient underground irrigation system that made Turpan's agricultural oasis possible. A network of vertical shafts connected by horizontal tunnels channels glacial meltwater from the Tianshan foothills to fields in the desert basin.
看朋友	kàn péng you	to visit a friend; to go see someone — 看 extends naturally from visual attention to social attention: going to see someone implies caring, checking in, tending to the relationship. 看朋友 (visit a friend), 看望 kànwàng (to visit and care for, especially an elder or patient), 探望 tànwàng (to pay a visit, more formal).
科学家	kē xu é jiā	scientist — 科学 kēxué (science, lit. "the study of branches/categories") + 家 jiā. One who has made science their home.
磕头	kē tóu	prostration — kowtow — the physical language of reverence — 磕头 (kowtow, lit. "knock head") is the full prostration: kneeling and touching the forehead to the floor.
客家人	Kè jiā rén	The Hakka people, literally "guest family people," so named because they arrived in the regions where they settled as migrants, after earlier inhabitants.
客人	kèr én	guest
客厅	kè tīng	living room
空姐	kōng jiě	flight attendant (female) — 空 (sky, air) plus 姐 is the colloquial word for a female flight attendant, literally a "sky sister." It is a clipped, affectionate everyday term beside the formal 空中小姐 (kōngzhōng xiǎojiě).

裤子	kù zi	trousers
快乐	kuài lè	happy; joyful; happiness (as a state) — 快 kuài (released; unobstructed) + 乐 lè (music; joy; ease). The standard word for happiness in modern Mandarin: it is an adjective predicate, a pre-noun modifier, and a noun. Appears in greetings and fixed phrases far more than the simple 快 alone.
筷子	kuài zi	chopsticks

老百姓	lǎo bǎi xìng	the common people; ordinary citizens — 老 (old/established) + 百 (hundred) + 姓 (surname; clan). Lit. "the hundred old clans": the established common families as opposed to the aristocracy. The standard term for ordinary people, used across the political spectrum.
老板	lǎo bǎn	boss; business owner — 老 lǎo + 板 bǎn (plank; board, extended to the person in charge). The everyday term for boss, owner, or the person in charge of a business. In restaurants, calling out 老板！is the standard way to get the owner's attention.
老大哥	lǎo dà gē	big brother (figure or nation)
老豆腐	lǎo dòu fu	Firm Tofu; Northern Tofu
老公	lǎo gōng	husband (colloquial) — 老 lǎo (old; familiar prefix) + 公 gōng (elder male). The warm, everyday word a woman uses for her husband, paired with 老婆 lǎopó (wife). More casual than 丈夫 zhàngfu.
老江湖	lǎo jiāng hú	a seasoned worldly person; a streetwise veteran; an old hand — 老 lǎo (old; experienced) + 江湖 (the wanderers' world). A person who has been around , who has seen the tricks, navigated the codes, survived the dangers, and accumulated knowledge with scars.
老练	lǎo liàn	seasoned, experienced — 老 (old) + 练 (tempered). Weathered by long practice into steadiness: a veteran's unflappable competence. Praises judgment and poise, not just technical speed.
老朋友	lǎo péng you	old friend; longtime friend — A friend of long standing. 老 lǎo (old) marks the length of the friendship, not the person's age. A warm word, often used on reunion: 老朋友了 ("we go way back"). The opposite, a new friend, is 新朋友 xīn péngyou.
老人节	lǎo rén jié	Elders Day; Senior Citizens Day — 老人 lǎorén (elderly person; old person) + 节 jié (festival; designated day). The official civic designation established in 1989, co-locating a state-recognized day honoring elderly citizens with the existing Double Ninth Festival.
老生	lǎo shēng	bearded male role
老师	lǎo shī	teacher — the most common honorific in Chinese education — 老 lǎo (venerable) + 师 shī (master; teacher; expert). The standard term for teacher at all levels, from elementary school through university. Used as a title of address (王老师 "Teacher Wang") and as a term of respect for anyone with expertise to transmit.
老实	lǎo shi	honest; well-behaved; simple and straightforward — 老 lǎo (established; true) + 实 shí (solid; real). Meaning cluster: (1) honest and straightforward (他很老实，不会骗人 "he's very honest, won't deceive anyone"); (2) well-behaved, obedient (孩子今天很老实 "the child is well-behaved today"); (3) naive, easy to take advantage of (别太老实了 "don't be too naive").
老实说	lǎo shí shuō	"Frankly" or "to be straight with you — "Frankly" or "to be straight with you." Close cousin to 说实话, with the same function. 老实 literally means "honest, straightforward." Slightly warmer in tone, often used among friends before offering an opinion the listener might not want to hear.
老天 · 老天爷	lǎo tiān lǎo tiān yé	dear heaven; good God (colloquial exclamation) — 老 lǎo (old; venerable, a term of respectful familiarity) + 天 tiān + 爷 yé (grandfather; lord). The folk personification of heaven as a kindly, or sometimes indifferent, old grandfather.
老子	lǎo zǐ	Laozi, the attributed author
