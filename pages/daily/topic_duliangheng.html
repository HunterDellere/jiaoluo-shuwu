度量衡

dùliànghéng

Fruit is priced by the half-kilo 斤, distance walks in 里, and an idiom about six of one and half a dozen still counts in base sixteen.

~5 min read
度量衡 dùliànghéng The Word
统一度量衡 tǒngyī dùliànghéng · The First Emperor's Standard

度量衡 (dùliànghéng) names the three classical domains of measurement in three characters: 度 (dù) is length, 量 (liàng) is volume, and 衡 (héng) is weight, originally the beam of a balance scale. The phrase is welded in Chinese historical memory to one act: after unifying the warring states in 221 BCE, the First Emperor of Qin decreed 统一度量衡, the standardization of weights and measures across the empire, alongside unified currency, axle widths, and script. Surviving bronze and pottery standard-measures stamped with the imperial edict are museum staples, and every Chinese schoolchild learns the standardization as part of what "unification" concretely meant: a bushel of grain owed in tax had to be the same bushel everywhere.

The units themselves stayed remarkably stable as names while their values drifted for two millennia, varying by dynasty, region, and trade. The modern settlement came in 1959, when the mainland fixed the market units (市制 shìzhì) as round conversions of the metric system. The old names survived because the government defined them instead of banning them.

斤与两 jīn yǔ liǎng The Market Units
市制 shìzhì · The Market System Today 斤 jīn → 500 g : the unit of every produce price ; 一斤苹果多少钱?
两 liǎng → 50 g : 10 per 斤 since 1959 (historically 16) ; portions of dumplings and baijiu
里 lǐ → 500 m : traditional distance ; alive in idioms and place names
亩 mǔ → ≈ 666.7 m² : farmland's unit ; 15 亩 = 1 hectare
尺 chǐ → ⅓ m : the "Chinese foot" ; 寸 cùn → ⅓ dm, the "Chinese inch"
All are defined as exact fractions of metric units, so conversion is mental arithmetic, not lookup
半斤八两 bàn jīn bā liǎng · Base Sixteen in an Idiom

斤 (jīn) began as a pictograph of an axe, and the axe sense survives as the character's radical duty in 断 (cut) and 新 (new, originally fresh-cut wood). As a weight it is the unit of daily life: market stalls, supermarket scales, and delivery apps all price food per 斤, and a learner's first market transaction runs through it. Body weight is discussed in 斤 too; a person 减肥 (dieting) counts progress in 斤, which flatters twice as fast as kilograms. The subdivision 两 (liǎng) portions small quantities: 二两饺子 is a modest plate of dumplings (about 100 grams of dough weight), and spirits are poured by the 两.

Until 1959 the 斤 held sixteen 两, a legacy of the balance-scale convenience of successive halving. The old base survives in two fixed phrases. 半斤八两 ("half a jin, eight liang") means two things are exactly equal, usually equally bad. And a person of conscience was said to have their 良心 weighed by the sixteen stars painted on the steelyard's beam: seven for the Dipper, six for the South Dipper, and three for fortune, prosperity, and longevity, so a merchant who shorted a customer by one liang was said to lose one of the three. The moralized steelyard (杆秤 gǎnchèng) is disappearing from markets in favor of digital scales, taking its folklore with it.

里与亩 lǐ yǔ mǔ Distance and Land
千里之行 qiān lǐ zhī xíng · The Li of Literature

里 (lǐ), now fixed at 500 meters, is the distance unit of the entire classical corpus, and its idioms are load-bearing: 千里之行始于足下 ("a journey of a thousand li begins beneath one's feet," from the Daodejing), 万里长城 (the ten-thousand-li Long Wall), 一日千里 ("a thousand li in a day," of blazing progress), and the courtesy 千里送鹅毛 ("a goose feather sent a thousand li," a small gift carrying long-distance sentiment). Everyday distance has gone metric, but the way to say kilometer, 公里, literally "standard li," keeps the old unit inside the new one. The homophone 里 meaning neighborhood or inside (as in 哪里, 这里) is a separate word that merged into the same simplified character, a famous ambiguity of simplification.

亩 (mǔ), roughly a fifteenth of a hectare, is how Chinese farmland has been counted from the well-field idealizations of Mencius to the yield statistics in this morning's agricultural news. Grain output is reported in 斤 per 亩, land transfers and rural policy documents run on the unit, and the phrase 一亩三分地 ("one mu and three fen of land"), originally the plot the Qing emperor ceremonially plowed, is the standard metaphor for one's own small turf: 别只顾自己的一亩三分地, don't tend only your own patch.

公制 gōngzhì The 公 Prefix
公斤、公里、公升 · Metric Wearing Local Clothes

When the metric system arrived, Chinese did not transliterate "kilogram" and "kilometer"; it minted 公斤 ("standard jin," 2 jin exactly) and 公里 ("standard li," 2 li exactly), using 公 (gōng, public, official) as a metric marker. The result is a bilingual measurement culture with clean mental arithmetic: doubling converts jin to kilograms, and halving converts back. Meters themselves are 米 (mǐ, a sound-loan that happens to be the character for rice), centimeters are 厘米, and height is stated in meters colloquially: 他一米八 ("he's 1.8 meters") is the standard way to say someone is tall, compressed to 一米八 with no unit repetition.

In practice the two systems partition daily life by domain. Food and body weight speak 斤; science, medicine, road signs, and fitness apps speak 公斤 and 公里; farmland speaks 亩; apartment sizes speak 平方米 (square meters, colloquially 平米 or just 平: 一套九十平的房子, a 90-square-meter apartment). Taiwan and Hong Kong kept older values for some units (a Hong Kong market 斤 is about 605 grams, the old treaty-port "catty"), so a recipe or a gold price quoted in 两 means different grams on different shores, a live detail for anyone shopping across the strait.

词汇 cíhuì Key Vocabulary
n 斤 jīn

The market half-kilo. 一斤多少钱 ("how much per jin?") is the essential market question. Originally a pictograph of an axe, which is still its job as a radical.

n 公斤 gōngjīn

Kilogram: the standard jin, exactly two market jin. Medical charts, gyms, and airline baggage allowances use it; the market next door does not.

n 米 mǐ

Meter, borrowed onto the rice character purely for sound. 一米八 is the shorthand for an enviable height; 百米 is the hundred-meter dash.

n 里 lǐ

The traditional half-kilometer, alive in idioms and the name of the Great Wall. 公里 is the kilometer built from it. Distinguish the merged homophone in 这里 (here).

n 亩 mǔ

The farmland unit, about 667 square meters. 一亩三分地 is everyone's metaphorical private turf, from an emperor's ceremonial plot.

v 称 chēng

To weigh. 称一下 ("weigh it, please") hands the fruit to the vendor. The same character read chèn means "to fit," as in 称心如意, suiting one's heart's wishes.

常见问题chángjiàn wèntíFrequently Asked Questions
How much is a 斤 (jīn)?
Exactly 500 grams in mainland China today, where it remains the standard market unit for food: fruit, vegetables, and meat are all priced per 斤. It divides into 10 两 (liǎng) of 50 grams each. Historically a 斤 had 16 两, which is where the idiom 半斤八两 comes from.
What does 半斤八两 (bàn jīn bā liǎng) mean?
Six of one, half a dozen of the other. Under the old 16-两 jin, half a jin was exactly eight liang, so the two quantities are identical. The idiom outlived the decimalization of the unit in 1959 and is usually mildly derogatory: both alternatives are equally mediocre.
How does Chinese say kilometer and kilogram?
With the prefix 公 ('public, standard'): 公里 (gōnglǐ, kilometer, 'standard li') and 公斤 (gōngjīn, kilogram, 'standard jin'). The metric system was mapped onto the traditional unit names, defining one metric unit as a round multiple of the old one.
