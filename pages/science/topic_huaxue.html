化学

huàxué

The Chinese periodic table is a naming machine: one character per element, with the radical telling you its state at room temperature.

~5 min read
化学 huàxué The Word
变化之学 biànhuà zhī xué · The Study of Transformation

化学 (huàxué) is "transformation-study." 化 (huà) is the language's general verb of change: it sits inside 变化 (biànhuà, change), 文化 (wénhuà, culture, "transformation by writing"), and 消化 (xiāohuà, digestion). Naming chemistry after transformation is more accurate than the European name, which still carries the fossil of alchemy. Chinese had its own alchemy, 炼丹 (liàndān, "refining the elixir"), the Daoist art that pursued immortality pills for emperors and, along the way, produced gunpowder; the modern discipline kept none of that vocabulary and started clean with 化.

The term itself entered Chinese in the 1850s through missionary translation work in Shanghai, and was then borrowed by Japanese (kagaku), one of the currents in the large two-way traffic of scientific vocabulary between the two languages in that half-century. From 化学 the compounds fall out mechanically: 化学家 (chemist), 化学反应 (chemical reaction), 化肥 (huàféi, chemical fertilizer), and the school-hallway split into 有机化学 (organic, "having-mechanism") and 无机化学 (inorganic).

元素周期表 yuánsù zhōuqībiǎo One Character per Element
部首定态 bùshǒu dìng tài · The Radical Encodes the State 钅 (metal radical) → metals : 钠 nà (sodium), 钙 gài (calcium), 铁 tiě (iron), 金 jīn (gold)
气 (gas radical) → gases : 氢 qīng (hydrogen), 氧 yǎng (oxygen), 氮 dàn (nitrogen), 氦 hài (helium)
石 (stone radical) → solid nonmetals : 碳 tàn (carbon), 硅 guī (silicon), 磷 lín (phosphorus), 碘 diǎn (iodine)
氵/水 (water) → liquids : 溴 xiù (bromine), 汞 gǒng (mercury)
Glance at any element character and you know its room-temperature state before you know its name
音与义 yīn yǔ yì · Sound Borrowed, Meaning Built

Inside each element character, the non-radical half usually approximates the international name: 钠 (nà) carries 内 for natrium, 镁 (měi) carries 美 for magnesium, 氦 (hài) carries 亥 for helium. A handful of the most common elements got meaning instead of sound, and these are small poems. 氢 (qīng, hydrogen) is built on 轻, "light," the lightest gas. 氧 (yǎng, oxygen) is built on 养, "to nourish," the gas that sustains. 氮 (dàn, nitrogen) is built on 淡, "dilute," the gas that thins the air's oxygen. 氯 (lǜ, chlorine) is built on 绿, "green," for the gas's visible color, exactly the etymology of "chlorine" from Greek chloros.

The system is productive to this day. When the seventh row of the periodic table was completed, the Chinese language authorities approved new characters for the new elements, including 鿬 (tián) for tennessine (element 117, confirmed 2016) and 鿫 (ào) for oganesson, each following the old formula of state radical plus phonetic. The Chinese periodic table is thus the world's only one where every entry is a single syllable and a single glyph, which makes formula recitation in a Chinese classroom (二氧化碳, "two-oxygen-transform-carbon," CO₂) read like grammar rather than nomenclature.

徐寿 Xú Shòu The Man Who Named the Metals
江南制造局 Jiāngnán Zhìzàojú · Translating at the Arsenal

Most of the metal characters were coined by one man. Xu Shou (徐寿, 1818–1884), a self-taught Qing polymath who built China's first steamship, spent his later career in the translation department of the Jiangnan Arsenal in Shanghai, working with the British missionary John Fryer. Their 1871 rendering of a Western chemistry textbook, 化学鉴原 (Huàxué Jiànyuán, "Mirror of the Origins of Chemistry"), needed names for dozens of elements Chinese had never distinguished, and Xu's solution was the formula still in use: take the metal radical 钅, attach a phonetic that approximates the Western sound, and mint a character. 镁, 钙, 锌 (xīn, zinc), and their kin are his.

It was a controversial move. Inventing characters wholesale ran against two thousand years of philological conservatism, and rival translation schemes proposed multi-character phonetic transcriptions instead (the way 咖啡 spells "coffee"). Xu's one-character system won because chemistry needs its atoms notationally atomic: 硫酸钠 (sodium sulfate) parses instantly as sulfur-acid-sodium, which no transcription scheme could match. His coinages are among the very few characters in the modern script whose inventor, date, and motive are all documented.

实验室与厨房 shíyànshì yǔ chúfáng Lab and Everyday Chemistry
从分子到味精 · From Molecules to MSG

The structural vocabulary is compact and transparent: 分子 (fēnzǐ, molecule, "divided unit"), 原子 (yuánzǐ, atom, "original unit"), 离子 (lízǐ, ion, "departed unit," for the particle that has left electrons behind), all sharing the noun-forming 子 of 电子 (diànzǐ, electron). A reaction is 反应 (fǎnyìng), an experiment is 实验 (shíyàn, "test against reality"), and the lab is the 实验室. Acids are 酸 (suān), which is also the everyday word for "sour," and bases are 碱 (jiǎn), so pH strips in a Chinese classroom test 酸碱度, "the degree of sour and alkaline."

Everyday life keeps the chemistry close to the surface. Vinegar is 醋 (cù) and its acidity is the same 酸 you taste; carbonated drinks are 汽水 (qìshuǐ, "gas water") containing 二氧化碳; the MSG in the kitchen is 味精 (wèijīng, "essence of flavor"), and the stern label 不含化学添加剂 ("contains no chemical additives") does the same rhetorical work it does in English. The word 化学 itself can carry a whiff of suspicion in food talk, and 纯天然 ("purely natural") is its marketing antonym.

词汇 cíhuì Key Vocabulary
n 化学 huàxué

Chemistry: transformation-study. 化学反应 is a chemical reaction; 化学式 (huàxuéshì) is a chemical formula, read left to right like grammar.

n 元素 yuánsù

Element: "original plain-stuff." Beyond the lab it means element in the design sense too: 中国元素 (Chinese elements) is the stock phrase for Chinese motifs in fashion or film.

n 氧气 yǎngqì

Oxygen (as breathable gas): the nourishing 氧 plus 气. 缺氧 (quēyǎng) is oxygen deprivation, used literally at altitude and figuratively for stuffy rooms and dull meetings.

n 分子 fēnzǐ

Molecule: divided unit. The same word, differently stressed, means "member of a group" in political vocabulary (知识分子, intellectuals), a coincidence of compounding worth knowing before it confuses.

v/n 实验 shíyàn

Experiment: to test against reality. 做实验 is to run an experiment; 实验室 is the laboratory; 实验中学 is the "experimental high school" found in nearly every Chinese city.

adj/n 酸 suān

Acid; sour. One word covers the chemical class and the taste, plus muscle soreness (腿酸, sore legs) and the sting of envy (心里酸酸的). Chemistry students never forget which taste an acid has.

常见问题chángjiàn wèntíFrequently Asked Questions
How are chemical elements named in Chinese?
Every element is exactly one character. The radical encodes its room-temperature state: 钅 (metal) for metals like 钠 sodium, 气 (gas) for gases like 氧 oxygen, 石 (stone) for solid nonmetals like 碳 carbon, and 氵 (water) for the liquid 溴 bromine. The other half of the character usually approximates the international name's sound.
Who created the Chinese element characters?
Most metal characters were coined by the Qing translator Xu Shou (徐寿), who rendered Western chemistry texts in the 1870s at the Jiangnan Arsenal and invented characters like 镁 (magnesium) and 钙 (calcium) by pairing the metal radical with a phonetic. The system he set up still absorbs new elements: China approved a new character for tennessine after its 2016 confirmation.
What does 化学 (huàxué) literally mean?
Transformation-study. 化 is the verb of change (the same one inside 变化 and 文化), so chemistry is named for what it studies: matter changing into other matter. A chemical reaction is 化学反应 (huàxué fǎnyìng).
