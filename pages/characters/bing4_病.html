字源zìyuánEtymology & Structure
字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight

病 = 疒 + 丙. The outer frame 疒 (the sickness radical) is the semantic component, and 丙 (bǐng) sitting inside it is the phonetic. 疒 itself is a compressed picture: a person lying down (人) tipped against a low bed or couch, with two short strokes that older paleographers read as drops of sweat or as the lines of the bedframe. Wherever 疒 appears as a radical, the word concerns being unwell: 疼 (téng) ache, 痛 (tòng) pain, 疲 (pí) fatigue, 疯 (fēng) madness. It is one of the most semantically transparent radicals in the script.

The phonetic 丙 carries the sound and only the sound. In the old Heavenly Stems counting system 丙 is the third stem, and on its own it has no medical meaning; it was selected because its Old Chinese reading was close to the reading of 病. The Shuōwén Jiězì glosses 病 plainly as 疾加也, an illness that has worsened, distinguishing it from the lighter 疾 (jí). In the earliest texts 疾 is the ordinary ailment and 病 is the grave one, the sickness that has crossed into danger.

From the concrete sense of bodily sickness the character extended outward to anything wrong or defective. A flaw in an argument, a fault in a person's character, a bug in a machine: all can be a 病. This is the same move English makes when it calls a society "sick" or a plan "ailing." The radical keeps the body in view even when the subject is abstract, which is why 毛病 (máobìng) covers both a chronic cough and an annoying habit.

Japanese borrowed the character as 病 (byō / yamai) with the identical sickness radical, and it anchors the everyday word 病気 (byōki, illness) and 病院 (byōin, hospital). The Korean reading is 병 (byeong). Across the whole Sinographic world the glyph names the same thing: a body laid up on a bed, no longer whole.

疾病jíbìngIllness Words · Falling Ill
生病shēng bìngto fall ill, get sick
V 动词 dòngcí
生 (shēng) means to give rise to or produce, so 生病 is literally "to produce illness," the standard verb for getting sick. It is a separable verb: an object or aspect marker can split the two halves.
他昨天生病了，没来上班。
Tā zuótiān shēng bìng le, méi lái shàngbān.
He fell ill yesterday and did not come to work.
孩子一生病，全家都紧张。
Háizi yì shēng bìng, quánjiā dōu jǐnzhāng.
The moment the child gets sick, the whole family tenses up.
病人bìng rénpatient, sick person
N 名词 míngcí
病 + 人 (rén, person): a sick person, the general word for a patient in a hospital or clinic. It names the person by their condition rather than their role, unlike 患者 (huànzhě), which is the more clinical register used in medical writing.
医生正在给病人看病。
Yīshēng zhèngzài gěi bìngrén kàn bìng.
The doctor is examining the patient.
辨析 biànxī · 病人 vs. 患者 病人 is everyday speech; 患者 is the formal term in news reports and hospital signage.
看病kàn bìngto see a doctor; to treat a patient
V 动词 dòngcí
看 (kàn) means to look at, so 看病 covers both sides of the consultation: a patient goes to 看病 (see a doctor about an illness) and a doctor 看病 (examines and treats the sick). Context fixes which side is meant.
我下午要去医院看病。
Wǒ xiàwǔ yào qù yīyuàn kàn bìng.
I have to go to the hospital this afternoon to see a doctor.
疾病jí bìngdisease, illness (formal)
N 名词 míngcí
疾 (jí) and 病 are near-synonyms paired into one formal noun. In classical usage 疾 was the slight ailment and 病 the serious one; fused together, 疾病 is the neutral cover term for disease in medical and official language.
心脏疾病是常见的健康问题。
Xīnzàng jíbìng shì chángjiàn de jiànkāng wèntí.
Heart disease is a common health problem.
毛病máobìngFaults & Defects · What Goes Wrong
毛病máo bìngdefect, flaw, bad habit; malfunction
N 名词 míngcí
毛 (máo) means fine hair or fur, here the trivial detail; 毛病 is a small fault that keeps recurring. It stretches across a machine that breaks down, a person's annoying habit, and a minor recurring ailment, all the everyday senses of "something is wrong with it."
这台电脑老出毛病。
Zhè tái diànnǎo lǎo chū máobìng.
This computer keeps malfunctioning.
他有个挑食的毛病。
Tā yǒu ge tiāoshí de máobìng.
He has a bad habit of being a picky eater.
辨析 biànxī · 毛病 vs. 缺点 毛病 is concrete and often fixable (a fault, a glitch); 缺点 (quēdiǎn) is an abstract shortcoming or weakness.
病情bìng qíngcondition of an illness
N 名词 míngcí
病 + 情 (qíng, situation, state): the state of a sickness, how the illness stands. Doctors use it to report whether a patient is stable, worsening, or improving.
病人的病情已经稳定了。
Bìngrén de bìngqíng yǐjīng wěndìng le.
The patient's condition has already stabilized.
病房bìng fánghospital ward, sickroom
N 名词 míngcí
病 + 房 (fáng, room): the room where patients are kept, a hospital ward. It pairs naturally with 病床 (bìngchuáng, hospital bed), bringing the original sickbed of the radical 疒 full circle.
他被送进了急诊病房。
Tā bèi sòng jìn le jízhěn bìngfáng.
He was taken into the emergency ward.
病原bìngyuánDisease Agents · Germ & Virus
病毒bìng dúvirus
N 名词 míngcí
病 + 毒 (dú, poison): a "sickness poison," the standard word for a biological virus and, by extension, a computer virus. The same term covers the flu and the malware on your hard drive.
流感是由病毒引起的。
Liúgǎn shì yóu bìngdú yǐnqǐ de.
The flu is caused by a virus.
我的电脑中了病毒。
Wǒ de diànnǎo zhòng le bìngdú.
My computer caught a virus.
病菌bìng jūngerm, pathogenic bacterium
N 名词 míngcí
病 + 菌 (jūn, bacterium, fungus): a disease-causing germ. Where 病毒 names a virus, 病菌 names the bacterial culprit, the everyday word a parent uses when telling a child to wash their hands.
饭前洗手可以减少病菌。
Fàn qián xǐshǒu kěyǐ jiǎnshǎo bìngjūn.
Washing your hands before meals reduces germs.
传染病chuán rǎn bìnginfectious disease
N 名词 míngcí
传染 (chuánrǎn, to infect, to pass on) + 病: a disease that spreads from one person to another. It is the headline word during any epidemic and the category label on public-health notices.
医院设有专门的传染病区。
Yīyuàn shè yǒu zhuānmén de chuánrǎnbìng qū.
The hospital has a dedicated infectious-disease zone.
成语chéngyǔIdioms & Set Phrases
病入膏肓bìng rù gāo huāngthe illness has entered the vitals: beyond cureFrom the Zuǒ Zhuàn: a duke dreams the disease hides in the 膏肓, the region between heart and diaphragm that no needle or medicine can reach. Used today for any situation, not only medical, that has decayed past saving.
无病呻吟wú bìng shēn yínto groan without being sick: to complain for no reasonA literary critic's jab at writers who manufacture sorrow they do not feel. Now applied to anyone who dramatizes trivial troubles or affects suffering for effect.
同病相怜tóng bìng xiāng liánsharing the same sickness, they pity each other: fellow sufferers sympathizeFrom the Wú Yuè Chūnqiū. People who endure the same hardship feel a natural bond, the way two patients in one ward understand each other without explanation.
药到病除yào dào bìng chúthe medicine arrives, the illness is gone: an instant, effective cureA praise phrase for a skilled doctor or a decisive remedy. Extended to any solution that fixes a problem cleanly and at once.
记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image

See the radical 疒 as a low wooden bed leaned up on its side against a wall, a sick person stretched along it with two beads of sweat sliding off the frame. That tilted bed is the sickness radical, and it is the first thing your eye should catch in 病. Whatever sits inside the bed, the body is laid up.

Inside this one lies 丙 (bǐng), the third Heavenly Stem, doing nothing but holding the sound. Treat 丙 as a faceless lodger you cannot quite identify: it tells you how to say the word, bìng, in a sharp falling tone, like a doctor's clipped diagnosis. The 4th tone drop mirrors the patient sinking back onto the pillow.

Then watch the bed travel. Add a person and you get 病人, someone laid up on it. Add poison and you get 病毒, the unseen thing that put them there. Add a room and you get 病房, a hall of these beds in a row. Add fine hairs, 毛病, and the bed shrinks to a nagging little flaw that will not stay fixed. Every compound is just a new visitor to the same sideways sickbed.
