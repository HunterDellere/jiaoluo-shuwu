字源zìyuánEtymology & Structure
字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight

危 stacks two bodies in a single vertical column: 危 = 厃 + 㔾. On top sits 厃 (yán), the jut of a cliff face, a rock ledge that hangs out over empty air. Beneath it crouches 㔾 (jié), the bent form of a kneeling person, knees folded, spine curled. A figure pressed against the foot of an overhang, with all that stone leaning out above the skull. That is the picture the glyph holds: a person caught under the precipice, the stone and the body read as one scene.

The cliff component does double duty. 厃 fixes the meaning, the high and dangerous edge, and it also lends the sound, so 危 is a pictophonetic where the semantic and phonetic load rest on the same top piece. The Shuōwén Jiězì reads the character as a person standing at a high place, in dread, and the felt logic is identical: height and fear braided together. Standing on a ledge and standing under one are the same vertigo seen from two sides.

From that physical precariousness the senses fan out. First the literal high place, surviving in classical lines where 危 simply means lofty or steep, a tower 危 against the sky. Then the danger that height implies, since anything that high can fall, and anything beneath it can be crushed. By the time the word reaches modern Mandarin the abstract sense has won: 危 is danger and peril, the verb to endanger, the adjective for anything teetering on the edge of collapse. The lofty reading lingers only in old poetry and a handful of fixed phrases.

Japanese keeps the same glyph in 危ない (abunai), the everyday word called out as a warning, watch out, dangerous. Korean reads it 위 (wi), embedded in 위험 (wiheom), the exact cognate of 危险. Across all three the character does one job: it marks the moment just before the fall.

危险wēixiǎnThe Named Danger
危险wēixiǎndanger; dangerous
Adj 形容词 xíngróngcíN 名词 míngcí
危 (danger) plus 险 (steep, perilous), a doubling of two height-and-hazard words into the standard everyday term for danger. Works as both adjective (this is dangerous) and noun (a danger).
这里很危险，别靠近。
Zhèlǐ hěn wēixiǎn, bié kàojìn.
It's very dangerous here, don't go near.
他冒着生命危险救了那个孩子。
Tā màozhe shēngmìng wēixiǎn jiùle nàge háizi.
He risked his life to save that child.
危害wēihàito harm; harm, hazard
V/N 动名 dòngmíng
危 (endanger) plus 害 (harm). The danger turned active: to damage or jeopardize something, and the resulting harm itself. Common with health, society, environment.
吸烟严重危害健康。
Xīyān yánzhòng wēihài jiànkāng.
Smoking seriously harms your health.
辨析 biànxī · 危害 vs. 危险 危险 names a state of danger; 危害 names an act of harm or its damage. Pollution 危害 (harms) the environment, and so the environment becomes 危险 (in danger).
危急wēijícritical; urgent and dangerous
Adj 形容词 xíngróngcí
危 (danger) plus 急 (urgent). Danger compressed by time: a situation that is both perilous and pressing, where a hospital patient or a battlefield hangs by a thread.
病人情况危急，必须马上手术。
Bìngrén qíngkuàng wēijí, bìxū mǎshàng shǒushù.
The patient's condition is critical; surgery is needed immediately.
危机wēijīThe Turning Point
词义洞见 cíyì dòngjiàn · Lexical Insight

危机 pairs 危 (danger) with 机 (the pivot, the mechanism, the moment a trigger releases). The popular Western reading, that the word means danger plus opportunity, is a romantic stretch: 机 here carries its sense of crux or critical juncture, not opportunity in the cheerful sense. Still, the glyph holds a real truth. A 危机 is the point where a situation is wound to its tightest, the spring that will either snap or fire. It is danger arrived at its decisive turn.

危机wēijīcrisis
N 名词 míngcí
危 (danger) plus 机 (critical juncture). A crisis: the decisive, threatening turn in an economy, a relationship, an illness, a career.
这场金融危机影响了全世界。
Zhè chǎng jīnróng wēijī yǐngxiǎngle quán shìjiè.
This financial crisis affected the whole world.
公司正面临严重的信任危机。
Gōngsī zhèng miànlín yánzhòng de xìnrèn wēijī.
The company is facing a serious crisis of trust.
危楼wēilóua dangerous building; a lofty tower
N 名词 míngcí
危 (here in both senses) plus 楼 (multi-storey building). In modern use, a condemned structure unsafe to enter. In classical poetry the same word means a tall tower rising into the air, the older lofty reading of 危.
那是一座危楼，已经被封了。
Nà shì yí zuò wēilóu, yǐjīng bèi fēng le.
That's a condemned building; it's already been sealed off.
辨析 biànxī · two readings 危楼高百尺 (a lofty tower a hundred feet high) is Lǐ Bái's tower in the sky; 危楼 on a demolition notice is a deathtrap. Same word, the two faces of 危.
安危ānwēisafety and danger; well-being
N 名词 míngcí
安 (safety) bound to 危 (danger) as one word: the whole question of whether someone is safe or in peril. A fixed pair where the two opposites name a single concern, often someone's fate or welfare.
他不顾个人安危，冲进了火场。
Tā búgù gèrén ānwēi, chōngjìnle huǒchǎng.
Disregarding his own safety, he rushed into the burning building.
成语chéngyǔIdioms & Set Phrases
危在旦夕wēi zài dàn xīdanger lies in dawn-and-dusk: peril is imminent, near deathSānguó Zhì era usage. 旦夕 is the span of a single day; the danger is so close it could fall within hours. Used for a dying patient or a regime about to collapse.
居安思危jū ān sī wēidwelling in safety, think of danger: stay alert in good timesFrom the Zuǒ Zhuàn. The classic counsel of prudent statecraft: when secure, prepare for the threat that has not yet come. A staple of formal and editorial Chinese.
危言耸听wēi yán sǒng tīngalarming words to startle the listener: scaremongeringHere 危 carries its older sense of high-pitched, alarming speech. Used to dismiss someone exaggerating a threat to frighten an audience. Mildly critical register.
转危为安zhuǎn wēi wéi ānturn danger into safety: pull through a crisisA four-character mirror of 居安思危, read in the other direction. The relief phrase after a patient stabilizes or a company survives. Common in news and speeches.
记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image

Picture a narrow mountain path. A traveler has dropped to his knees on a stone ledge, hands flat against the rock, because directly above him the cliff face juts out, a slab of grey stone overhanging his bent back. He does not look up. He waits, folded small, while a thread of gravel hisses down past his ear. That crouched body is 㔾, the kneeling figure at the bottom of the glyph; the overhang above is 厃, the jut of cliff that both names the height and gives the word its sound.

Hold that frame and the whole family falls into place. Pair the danger with 险 (steepness) and you get 危险, the everyday word: this ledge is dangerous. Wind the moment to its breaking point, the spring about to fire, and the gravel becomes a 危机, the crisis at its turn. Add time pressure, a rockslide already loosening, and the situation is 危急. Let the rock actually strike and you have 危害, harm done.

And remember the older face hidden in the same stone: 危 once meant simply high. The very ledge that endangers the traveler is, seen from the valley, a 危楼, a tower lifting into the clear air. Height and dread are one glyph because they are one feeling, the body's lurch at the edge of the fall.
