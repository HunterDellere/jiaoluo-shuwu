字源zìyuánEtymology & Structure
字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight

喝 = 口 + 曷. The left side is 口 (kǒu), an open mouth, drawn since the oracle bones as a simple gaping square. The right side is 曷 (hé), which carries no meaning here; it lends only its sound. This is the classic pictophonetic build of the 口 family: a semantic mouth on the left, a phonetic cue on the right, the whole character pointing at something that happens at the lips.

The phonetic 曷 is itself a busy little glyph, 日 (sun) over 匃 (to beg), and in older texts it worked as an interrogative, roughly "why, when, how". None of that survives in 喝. What carries over is the rime: 曷 hé and 喝 hē sit in the same phonetic series, so the right half tells a literate reader how the character sounds long before it tells them anything about water or wine.

The 口 radical does the semantic work, and it does it generously across the whole script. Put a mouth beside a phonetic and you get an act performed by the mouth: 吃 (chī) to eat, 吹 (chuī) to blow, 唱 (chàng) to sing, 喊 (hǎn) to yell. 喝 belongs to this crowd twice over. Read hē it is the mouth taking liquid in; read hè it is the mouth pushing sound out, a shout or a stern cry. Both readings are the same picture from opposite directions: the open mouth as gateway.

The 喝 (hè) shout reading is the older literary one and shows up in phrases like 大喝一声 (dà hè yī shēng), to bellow once. Everyday modern Mandarin overwhelmingly uses the hē reading for drinking; the hè reading survives mainly in fixed expressions and narrative prose. Japanese borrowed the same graph as 喝 (katsu), the sharp Zen shout a master barks to jolt a student awake, preserving exactly the "cry out" sense.

饮品yǐnpǐnEveryday Drinks
喝 + [liquid]: the grammar is flat and reliable. 喝 always takes the drink directly, no measure word required for the bare verb-object pair: 喝水, 喝茶, 喝酒, 喝汤 (hē tāng, drink soup). Note that Chinese says 喝汤 for soup, where English says "eat soup".
喝水hē shuǐto drink water
VO 动宾 dòngbīn
喝 (drink) + 水 (water). The first sentence most learners build with this character, and the everyday reminder a Chinese host or parent will repeat constantly.
多喝水，对身体好。
Duō hē shuǐ, duì shēntǐ hǎo.
Drink more water, it's good for you.
喝茶hē cháto drink tea
VO 动宾 dòngbīn
喝 (drink) + 茶 (tea). 喝茶 also names the social ritual of sitting down together; "请喝茶" is both an offer of tea and an invitation to talk.
我们找个地方喝茶聊天吧。
Wǒmen zhǎo ge dìfang hē chá liáotiān ba.
Let's find somewhere to drink tea and chat.
辨析 biànxī · 喝茶 vs. 品茶 喝茶 is just drinking tea; 品茶 (pǐn chá) is tasting tea attentively, the connoisseur's verb.
喝酒hē jiǔto drink alcohol
VO 动宾 dòngbīn
喝 (drink) + 酒 (liquor). The default verb for drinking alcohol of any kind, from beer to baijiu. Central to banquet culture, where 喝酒 carries social obligation.
开车不喝酒，喝酒不开车。
Kāichē bù hē jiǔ, hē jiǔ bù kāichē.
If you drive don't drink, if you drink don't drive.
喝咖啡hē kāfēito drink coffee
VO 动宾 dòngbīn
喝 (drink) + 咖啡 (coffee). Like 喝茶, the phrase doubles as a soft social invitation: "改天一起喝咖啡" means let's meet up sometime.
我早上不喝咖啡就没精神。
Wǒ zǎoshang bù hē kāfēi jiù méi jīngshén.
Without coffee in the morning I have no energy.
状态zhuàngtàiDrinking States & Manner
喝醉hē zuìto get drunk
V 动词 dòngcí
喝 (drink) + 醉 (drunk), a resultative compound: the 醉 is the result of the 喝. Often expanded to 喝醉了 (hē zuì le) for the completed state, "got drunk".
他昨天晚上喝醉了，什么都不记得。
Tā zuótiān wǎnshang hē zuì le, shénme dōu bù jìde.
He got drunk last night and remembers nothing.
喝完hē wánto finish drinking
V 动词 dòngcí
喝 (drink) + 完 (finished), another resultative: drink to completion, drain the glass. 完 marks the liquid as gone.
把这碗汤喝完吧。
Bǎ zhè wǎn tāng hē wán ba.
Finish this bowl of soup.
好喝hǎo hētasty (to drink)
ADJ 形容词 xíngróngcí
好 (good) + 喝 (drink): pleasant to drink. Mandarin splits "tasty" by mouth-action, so liquids are 好喝 while solid food is 好吃 (hǎo chī), good to eat.
这个奶茶真好喝。
Zhège nǎichá zhēn hǎo hē.
This milk tea is really tasty.
辨析 biànxī · 好喝 vs. 好吃 好喝 is for anything you drink; 好吃 (hǎo chī) is for anything you chew. Pick the verb by how it enters the mouth.
成语chéngyǔIdioms & Set Phrases
大喝一声dà hè yī shēngto give a great shout: bellow once, loudlyHere 喝 takes its hè reading, "to shout". A stock phrase in classical fiction and storytelling, the warrior or judge who freezes a room with a single roar.
吃喝玩乐chī hē wán lèeat, drink, play, be merry: a life of indulgenceFour verbs of pleasure strung together, with 喝 as the drinking. Usually mildly disapproving, said of someone who does nothing but enjoy themselves.
大吃大喝dà chī dà hēto eat and drink lavishly: feast extravagantlyThe doubled 大 stresses excess. Modern usage often flags wasteful banqueting or living beyond one's means.
喝西北风hē xīběi fēngto drink the northwest wind: have nothing to eat, go hungryA vivid colloquial idiom: with no food, all you can swallow is the cold wind. Said wryly of poverty or a failed livelihood, "我们都去喝西北风了".
记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image

Picture a wide-open mouth at a teahouse table, the 口 on the left of the character literally drawn as that opening. To its right stands 曷, a friend leaning in asking "why, when, how", the old questioning word, but here he says nothing meaningful; he is only there to whisper the sound hē into your ear. The mouth is doing all the work, the question-mark friend is just the pronunciation tag.

Now run the same mouth in both directions. Tip it back and liquid pours in: 喝水, 喝茶, 喝酒, all the way to 喝醉 when the wine wins. Open it the other way and sound rushes out: 大喝一声, the bellowed hè that silences the room. One glyph, one open mouth, two flows, in for drinking and out for shouting.

To lock the everyday reading, anchor it to thirst: 渴 (kě) dries your throat, 喝 (hē) wets it. They look almost identical because both lean on 曷; only the left radical differs, water 氵 for the thirst, mouth 口 for the act that fixes it.
