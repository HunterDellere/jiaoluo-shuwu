字源zìyuánEtymology & Structure
字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight

牛 is a pictograph of an ox's head seen straight on, the curved horns sweeping up and out from the crown. In the oracle-bone script the two horns flare to the top, the ears jut sideways, and a long line drops through the middle for the muzzle and snout. The seal script squares those horns into the top stroke and that vertical spine that survives untouched in the four strokes you write today: the slanted top, the long crossbar, the rising horn-tip, and the descending muzzle.

The Shuōwén Jiězì reads the glyph as the animal entire, a creature of weight and patience, and groups under it the whole vocabulary of the herd. 牛 is its own radical, the semantic flag on any character that has to do with cattle: 物 (wù, thing, originally a mottled ox), 特 (tè, special, once a single bull set apart for sacrifice), and 牧 (mù, to herd, an ox beside a hand holding a switch).

The horns are the giveaway. Set 牛 beside its near-twin 午 (wǔ) and the eye learns the difference: 牛 keeps the upward kick on its top-left stroke, the horn that no other common character carries. That single rising tick is the ox's signature.

The same head crossed into Japanese as ぎゅう / うし and into Korean as 우 (u), still meaning ox throughout the Sinographic world, still the radical for everything bovine.

饮食yǐnshíOn the Table: Dairy & Meat
牛奶niúnǎimilk (cow's)
N 名词 míngcí
牛 (ox/cow) + 奶 (nǎi, milk/breast). The default word for drinking milk in Mandarin; 奶 alone leans toward breast milk or the act of nursing, so the 牛 makes the source explicit.
我每天早上喝一杯牛奶。
Wǒ měitiān zǎoshang hē yì bēi niúnǎi.
I drink a glass of milk every morning.
牛肉niúròubeef
N 名词 míngcí
牛 (ox) + 肉 (ròu, meat). Chinese names meat by its animal, so beef is literally ox-meat, parallel to 猪肉 (zhūròu, pork) and 鸡肉 (jīròu, chicken).
这家面馆的牛肉面很有名。
Zhè jiā miànguǎn de niúròu miàn hěn yǒumíng.
This noodle shop's beef noodles are famous.
牛排niúpáisteak
N 名词 míngcí
牛 (ox) + 排 (pái, a slab/cutlet, from 排骨 spare-rib). A Western-style cut of beef cooked as one piece, ordered by doneness the way English orders rare to well-done.
我的牛排要七分熟。
Wǒ de niúpái yào qī fēn shú.
I'd like my steak medium-well.
蜗牛wōniúsnail
N 名词 míngcí
蜗 (wō, the snail's shell, with the 虫 insect radical) + 牛. The little horns of a snail earned it the ox; the word doubles as a byword for anything slow.
他做事慢得像蜗牛一样。
Tā zuòshì màn de xiàng wōniú yíyàng.
He works as slow as a snail.
引申yǐnshēnFigurative: Slang & Idiom
用法 yòngfǎ · Usage Note

On the street 牛 swings loose from the animal. Said of a person or a feat, 牛 (or the fuller 牛逼 niúbī, vulgar but everywhere) means impressively strong, gutsy, top-tier. The ox lends its raw power and stubbornness to the praise. Use 牛 freely; keep 牛逼 for friends and informal speech.

吹牛chuīniúto brag, to talk big
V/N 动名 dòngmíng
吹 (chuī, to blow) + 牛 (ox). From the full phrase 吹牛皮 (chuī niúpí, blow up an ox-hide), once said of herders inflating a hide skin-raft by lung power: big puff, little substance.
别吹牛了,你根本没去过。
Bié chuīniú le, nǐ gēnběn méi qùguò.
Quit bragging, you've never even been there.
辨析 biànxī · 吹牛 vs. 牛 吹牛 is boasting beyond the truth, a fault. 牛 on its own is genuine praise. One inflates the hide; the other admires the beast.
黄牛huángniúox; ticket scalper
N 名词 míngcí
黄 (huáng, yellow) + 牛, literally the yellow ox of the fields. In cities it became slang for a ticket scalper, from the way scalpers swarm and jostle like a milling herd.
演唱会的票全被黄牛抢光了。
Yǎnchànghuì de piào quán bèi huángniú qiǎngguāng le.
The concert tickets were all snatched up by scalpers.
牛仔裤niúzǎikùjeans
N 名词 míngcí
牛仔 (niúzǎi, cowboy, literally ox-lad) + 裤 (kù, trousers). The denim of the American cowboy, named in Chinese after the herder himself.
她今天穿了一条蓝色牛仔裤。
Tā jīntiān chuānle yì tiáo lánsè niúzǎikù.
She's wearing a pair of blue jeans today.
成语chéngyǔIdioms & Set Phrases
对牛弹琴duì niú tán qínto play the lute to an ox: to address the wrong audienceFrom a Han-dynasty Buddhist text, the Lǐhuò Lùn. The ox hears the music but takes nothing in. Said when fine words are wasted on someone who cannot grasp them, often with a touch of self-irony.
九牛一毛jiǔ niú yì máoone hair from nine oxen: a trifling fractionFrom Sīmǎ Qiān's letter to Rèn Ān in the Shǐjì. A single hair plucked from nine whole oxen, an amount so small it counts for nothing. Modern usage marks any negligible share of a vast whole.
汗牛充栋hàn niú chōng dòngto sweat the oxen and fill the rafters: a vast number of booksCoined by the Tang writer Liǔ Zōngyuán. So many volumes that hauling them makes the oxen sweat and stacking them fills the house to the roofbeams. Praise for a great library or prolific output.
牛刀小试niú dāo xiǎo shìa small test of the ox-cleaver: great talent on a minor taskFrom the cook Páo Dīng of the Zhuāngzǐ, whose ox-carving blade glides through every joint. A heavy cleaver lightly tried, said when a capable person handles an easy job and shows the skill in reserve.
记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image

Stand in front of the ox and look it dead in the face. Two horns curve up and out from the top of the skull, the broad muzzle drops straight down between them, and the ears flick to either side. That frontal head is the whole character: the slanted top stroke and the long crossbar are the brow and horns, the vertical that drops through them is the snout.

Hold onto the upward kick on the top-left, the one horn that rises. That tick is what keeps 牛 from collapsing into 午 (wǔ, noon). When you write the ox, flick that horn up first, then lay the crossbar of the brow, then drop the muzzle.

From that head the rest follows by sense. Milk and meat hang off it on the table (牛奶, 牛肉); blow the hide up too big and you are bragging (吹牛); and when someone is genuinely powerful, you call them 牛, the beast itself, all stubborn muscle and forward weight.
