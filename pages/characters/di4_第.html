字源 zìyuán Etymology & Structure
字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight

第 dì = ⺮ (zhú, bamboo) over 弟 (dì, younger brother). The bamboo radical sits on top as the meaning element, and 弟 below carries the sound. But 弟 is doing double duty here. Beyond marking the pronunciation, 弟 already pictured a cord wound in even turns around a stake, an image of regular, stepped order, which is exactly why the younger brother (the next in line of siblings) took that glyph. Add bamboo, whose nodes climb the stalk in even joints one above the next, and you get 第: things arranged in fixed sequence, step after step.

From that core image of ordered steps come the senses that anchor the character:

1. The ordinal prefix. Placed before a number, 第 turns a count into a position in a series: 第一 dì-yī (the first), 第二 dì-èr (the second), 第三 dì-sān (the third). This is the everyday job of 第 and the reason it appears on nearly every page of Chinese a learner reads.
2. Grade, rank, order. A standalone noun for one place in a graded series: 等第 děngdì (grade, rank), and the examination sense below.
3. Examination success. In the imperial system, to place in the civil-service exam: 及第 jídì (to pass and be ranked), 落第 luòdì (to fail).
4. A grand residence. By extension, the ranked mansion granted to or built by an official: 府第 fǔdì (a mansion), 宅第 zháidì (a stately home).

The thread through every sense is sequence: the bamboo nodes counting upward, the numbered place in a list, the rank earned in an exam, the graded standing of a great house. Wherever there is an order with fixed steps, 第 marks the step.

第一 dì-yī The Ordinal Prefix: Count into Order
语法洞见 yǔfǎ dòngjiàn · Grammatical Insight

The single most useful thing 第 does is convert a cardinal number into an ordinal. Chinese has no separate words for "first, second, third" the way English does; instead it sets 第 in front of the plain count. 一 yī is "one," and 第一 dì-yī is "the first." 二 èr is "two," and 第二 dì-èr is "the second." The rule never changes: 第 + number = the Nth in the series. 第十 is tenth, 第一百 is hundredth.

To name a specific item, a measure word usually follows: 第一个 dì-yī ge (the first one), 第一本书 dì-yī běn shū (the first book), 第三课 dì-sān kè (lesson three). Two very common fixed forms drop straight into daily speech: 第一次 dì-yī cì (the first time) and 第一名 dì-yī míng (first place, the winner).

To ask about position rather than state it, swap the number for 几 jǐ (how many / which): 第几 dì jǐ asks "which in order?" 你住第几层 "which floor do you live on?" 这是第几次 "what number time is this?" The answer comes back as another 第 form: 第八层 (the eighth floor), 第三次 (the third time).

第一 dì-yī first; number one
ORD 序数 xùshù
第 dì (ordinal) + 一 yī (one). The ordinal "first." Note the live tone-sandhi: 一 yī shifts to second tone before a fourth-tone syllable, so it is read dì-yí in 第一个 but stays dì-yī before a pause or another tone.
他是班里的第一名。
Tā shì bān lǐ de dì-yī míng.
He is the top of the class.
这是我第一次来中国。
Zhè shì wǒ dì-yī cì lái Zhōngguó.
This is my first time coming to China.
辨析 biànxī · 第一 vs. 一 一 yī is the cardinal, the bare count "one": 一本书 "one book." 第一 dì-yī is the ordinal, the position "first": 第一本书 "the first book." Drop 第 and you are counting how many; add it and you are naming which place in line.
第二 dì-èr second; number two
ORD 序数 xùshù
第 dì (ordinal) + 二 èr (two). The ordinal "second." The same pattern extends without limit: 第三 third, 第四 fourth, 第十 tenth. The number after 第 is just the ordinary cardinal, unchanged.
请翻到第二页。
Qǐng fān dào dì-èr yè.
Please turn to the second page.
第几 dì jǐ which (in order); what number in a series
INT 疑问 yíwèn
第 dì (ordinal) + 几 jǐ (how many; which). The question form that asks for a position rather than a quantity. The expected answer is always another 第 form: 第三 (the third), 第七 (the seventh).
你是第几个到的？
Nǐ shì dì jǐ ge dào de?
Which number were you to arrive?
第一 dì-yī First & Foremost: the Best of All
语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight

Because the first place in any ranking is the top one, 第一 dì-yī slides easily from the neutral "first" into the evaluative "foremost, the very best, above all else." 世界第一 shìjiè dì-yī is "first in the world," that is, the world's best. 天下第一 tiānxià dì-yī, "first under heaven," is the classic boast of unmatched supremacy, the title a martial-arts master or a great dish or a famous scenic spot might claim.

The same 第一 also serves as a strong adverbial "above all, first and foremost," ranking a priority rather than a thing: 安全第一 ānquán dì-yī, "safety first," puts safety at the head of the list. Here 第一 has left counting behind entirely and become a way of saying "this matters most."

世界第一 shìjiè dì-yī first in the world; the world's best
N 名词 míngcí
世界 shìjiè (world) + 第一 dì-yī (first). The top of a global ranking, by extension the finest there is. Used of athletes, products, and achievements that lead the field.
她是世界第一的网球选手。
Tā shì shìjiè dì-yī de wǎngqiú xuǎnshǒu.
She is the world's number-one tennis player.
安全第一 ānquán dì-yī safety first; safety above all
EXPR 短语 duǎnyǔ
安全 ānquán (safety) + 第一 dì-yī (first). A fixed slogan ranking safety as the top priority. Here 第一 means "the foremost concern," not a numbered position.
开车的时候，安全第一。
Kāichē de shíhou, ānquán dì-yī.
When you drive, safety comes first.
第一流 dì-yī liú first-rate; first-class; top-tier
ADJ 形容词 xíngróngcí
第一 dì-yī (first) + 流 liú (stream; class). Of the highest grade or quality: 第一流的大学 "a first-rate university." A close synonym of 一流 yīliú, with 第 adding a touch of formality.
这是一所第一流的大学。
Zhè shì yī suǒ dì-yī liú de dàxué.
This is a first-rate university.
及第 jídì Rank & Residence: the Classical 第
历史洞见 lìshǐ dòngjiàn · Historical Insight

Before 第 settled into its modern job as a colorless prefix, it was a full noun meaning a graded place in a ranking, and it carried two weighty senses out of imperial life. The first is the examination. For more than a thousand years the civil-service exam (科举 kējǔ) ranked candidates in strict order, and to place in that order was 及第 jídì, literally "to reach a rank." Its opposite, to fail, was 落第 luòdì, "to fall from the rank." A candidate who took the very top place was 及第 of the highest grade, a life-changing result that could lift an entire family.

The second sense is the mansion. Officials of rank were granted or built graded residences, and the house itself came to be called a 第, a "ranked dwelling": 府第 fǔdì (an official's mansion), 宅第 zháidì or 宅邸 (a stately home), 门第 méndì (the standing of a family, literally "gate and house," that is, lineage and rank). These uses are now literary and historical rather than everyday, but they reveal the older weight behind a character a learner first meets as a humble numbering tool.

及第 jídì to pass the imperial examination; to place in the rankings
V 动词 dòngcí
及 jí (to reach; to attain) + 第 dì (rank). To succeed in the civil-service exam and be placed in the official order. A historical and literary term; the modern equivalent is 考上 kǎoshàng (to test into).
他终于金榜及第。
Tā zhōngyú jīnbǎng jídì.
At last he passed the examination and made the gold roll.
府第 fǔdì a mansion; an official's grand residence
N 名词 míngcí
府 fǔ (official residence; manor) + 第 dì (ranked dwelling). The stately home of a high official or noble family. Literary and historical; you meet it in novels and on heritage plaques rather than in conversation.
这座古老的府第保存得很好。
Zhè zuò gǔlǎo de fǔdì bǎocún de hěn hǎo.
This ancient mansion is very well preserved.
成语 chéngyǔ Idioms & Set Phrases
及第成名 jí dì chéng míng to pass the exam and make a name: to win rank and renown at once Lit: reach-rank-become-famous. The double prize of the imperial scholar, to place in the examination and so earn lasting reputation. The dream that drove generations of candidates to the 科举.
登科及第 dēng kē jí dì to ascend the rolls and place in the rankings: to succeed in the civil-service exam Lit: mount-the-roster-reach-rank. 登科 is to have one's name posted on the pass list, 及第 to be ranked within it. A set phrase of congratulation for examination success.
记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image

Picture a stalk of bamboo, ⺮, growing upward in even joints, one node stacked above the next. Each joint is a fixed step in a sequence, and that is the whole idea of 第: order with numbered places. Below the bamboo sits 弟, the younger brother, the next sibling in line, lending both the sound and the same sense of one-after-another.

So whenever you see 第 in front of a number, read it as "the node, the step, the place": 第一 is the first joint, 第二 the second, 第三 the third. Ask 第几 and you are asking which joint up the stalk. And in the old imperial world, to be 及第 was to reach a joint on the ranked ladder of the exam, to live in a 府第 was to hold a graded place among the great houses. Bamboo counting upward, step by ordered step: that is 第.
