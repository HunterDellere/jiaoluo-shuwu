字源 zìyuán Etymology & Structure
字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight

The modern character is a clean phono-semantic compound: 星 = 日 + 生, the sun radical 日 above as the meaning component (a celestial point of light) and 生 (shēng) below lending the sound. The early history is more crowded with light. Oracle-bone and seal forms wrote multiple star-dots, small squares or circles scattered like a constellation, often three of them, the same idea preserved in 晶 (jīng), the three-sun character that originally depicted starlight and now means "crystal" and "sparkling." The phonetic 生 was added to pin down the reading, and the dots gradually consolidated into a single 日.

There is a quiet poetry in the surviving anatomy: 生 is the sprout-breaking-ground character, life emerging, so the settled form reads to the modern eye as light born above growth. That is folk etymology rather than philology, but it is exactly the kind of accidental image that makes a character memorable: the sky's lights over the growing world. The choice of 日 as radical also files the stars, correctly, in the sun's own drawer: the dictionary knew they were the same kind of thing long before astrophysics agreed.

星 extends from astronomical bodies to anything star-like: points of light (火星, sparks flying off a grinder), scattered small things (零星, "scattered bits"), and prominent people (明星, the bright stars of stage and screen). The measure word for stars is 颗 (kē), the classifier for small round things, so a wish is made on 一颗星.

星期 xīngqī How 星 Named the Week
七曜 qīyào · The Seven Luminaries

Traditional China counted days in ten-day 旬 (xún) cycles and by the sexagenary stems and branches; the seven-day week was an import. It arrived during the Tang dynasty with Buddhist and Central Asian astronomy as the cycle of the 七曜 (qīyào), the seven luminaries: sun, moon, and the five visible planets, one presiding over each day, the same scheme behind Sunday, Monday, and (via Norse substitution) the rest of the English week. Japan borrowed that system wholesale and still uses it: Japanese Monday is 月曜日, moon-day. Chinese let the luminary names lapse and, when the modern calendar was standardized in the last years of the Qing, adopted 星期 ("star period") as the official word for the week, numbering the days instead of naming them: 星期一 (Monday, "star-period one") through 星期六, with Sunday as 星期天 or the more formal 星期日.

Two rival week-words survive in daily speech, each carrying its history. 礼拜 (lǐbài, "worship") came in with Christian and Muslim observance, so 礼拜天 is Sunday by way of church; it remains common and colloquial, especially in the south. 周 (zhōu, "cycle") is the compact written choice: 周一 through 周日, 周末 for the weekend, 下周 for next week. A learner needs all three passively and can output whichever feels natural; the numbers do the real work, and only Sunday breaks the pattern, in every one of the three systems.

词汇 cíhuì Key 星 Compounds
星期 xīngqī week; day of the week
N 名词 míngcí
星 (star) + 期 (period): the star period, from the seven-luminary week. Days attach numbers: 星期三 is Wednesday. Asking the day is 今天星期几? , one of the few questions where 几 needs no measure word.
今天星期几？星期五。
Jīntiān xīngqī jǐ? Xīngqīwǔ.
What day is it today? Friday.
明星 míngxīng star (celebrity)
N 名词 míngcí
明 (bright) + 星: the bright star, classical Chinese for Venus before it became the word for screen and stage fame. 追星 ("chasing stars") is fandom, and a 粉丝 (fěnsī, fan, a sound-loan that also means glass noodles) does the chasing.
她从小的梦想就是当电影明星。
Tā cóngxiǎo de mèngxiǎng jiùshì dāng diànyǐng míngxīng.
Her dream since childhood has been to become a movie star.
火星 huǒxīng Mars; a spark
N 名词 míngcí
The fire star twice over: the red planet of the five-phase planet names, and the everyday spark from a fire or a grinder's wheel. Internet slang added a third life: a 火星人 (Martian) is someone hopelessly behind on the news.
中国的祝融号在火星上工作了一年多。
Zhōngguó de Zhùróng hào zài Huǒxīng shàng gōngzuò le yì nián duō.
China's Zhurong rover worked on Mars for over a year.
卫星 wèixīng satellite (natural or artificial)
N 名词 míngcí
卫 (guard) + 星: the guard star that attends a planet. Covers the moon, the GPS constellation, and the dish antenna (卫星天线). The Great Leap Forward idiom 放卫星 ("launching a satellite") still means announcing an inflated achievement.
北斗卫星导航系统覆盖全球。
Běidǒu wèixīng dǎoháng xìtǒng fùgài quánqiú.
The Beidou satellite navigation system covers the whole globe.
星座 xīngzuò constellation; zodiac sign
N 名词 míngcí
星 + 座 (seat): the seats of the stars. In young urban small talk it means the Western zodiac sign: 你是什么星座的? has largely displaced asking one's Chinese zodiac animal among the under-forty, an ironic import given the antiquity of native Chinese astral lore.
你是什么星座的？我是天蝎座。
Nǐ shì shénme xīngzuò de? Wǒ shì Tiānxiē zuò.
What's your star sign? I'm a Scorpio.
成语 chéngyǔ Idioms & Set Phrases
星罗棋布 xīng luó qí bù arrayed like stars, spread like chess pieces — dotted everywhere 星罗 (netted like stars) + 棋布 (laid out like go stones). The standard descriptor for anything scattered densely across a landscape: lakes on a delta, islands in an archipelago, convenience stores in Shanghai. The two images, night sky and go board, are both grids the classical eye knew intimately.
披星戴月 pī xīng dài yuè cloaked in stars, capped with the moon — toiling from before dawn until after dark To wear the stars as a cape and the moon as a hat is to be outdoors working while they are up. Said admiringly of farmers, delivery riders, and anyone on the early-and-late grind: 他披星戴月地工作，就为了孩子上大学。He works from starlight to moonlight, all so his child can attend university.
物换星移 wù huàn xīng yí things change, stars shift — time passes and the world turns over From Wang Bo's Tang-dynasty Pavilion of Prince Teng poem: 物换星移几度秋, "things change, stars shift, how many autumns now?" The stars' slow precession as the clock of dynasties. Used for nostalgia at scale: returning to a transformed hometown, rereading an old diary.
星星之火，可以燎原 xīngxing zhī huǒ, kěyǐ liáoyuán a single spark can set the prairie ablaze 星星 here is the spark sense of 星. The image of fire racing across a plain is ancient (the Book of Documents already warns of fire on the grassland), but the phrase's modern fame comes from Mao Zedong's 1930 essay taking it as a title, arguing a small revolutionary force could ignite the country. Now used for any small beginning with outsized potential, startups included, usually with the political echo fully intact.

常见问题chángjiàn wèntíFrequently Asked Questions
What does 星 (xīng) mean in Chinese?
Star, and by extension any point of light or prominence: planets (火星 Mars), satellites (卫星), sparks (火星 again, in its everyday sense), and celebrities (明星, 'bright star'). It is HSK 1, first tone, nine strokes, radical 日.
Why does the week use 星期 (xīngqī, 'star period') in Chinese?
The seven-day week reached Tang China with the seven luminaries (七曜): sun, moon, and the five visible planets, one per day. When the modern week was standardized at the end of the Qing, the term 星期 ('star period') was chosen, with days numbered 星期一 through 星期六 and Sunday as 星期天 or 星期日.
What is the structure of the character 星?
星 = 日 + 生: the sun radical 日 on top as the meaning component (a point of celestial light) and 生 (shēng) below as the phonetic. Early forms wrote several star-dots, sometimes three 日 shapes as in 晶, before settling on one.
