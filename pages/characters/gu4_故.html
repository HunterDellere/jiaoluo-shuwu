字源zìyuánEtymology & Structure
字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight

故 is a pictophonetic compound (形声字 xíngshēngzì): 古 gǔ (old, ancient) carries the sound, and 攵 pū (the strike radical, a variant of 攴, a hand holding a stick or rod) carries a sense of action done to something. The phonetic 古 is not silent here. It also leans on the page semantically, because 古 itself means "old, of long ago," and that meaning bleeds into nearly every sense 故 takes. The character keeps the same shape in simplified and traditional writing.

The Shuo Wen Jie Zi, the 2nd-century CE character dictionary, glosses 故 with 使为之也 (shǐ wéi zhī yě): roughly, "that which causes a thing to be done." This is the anchoring sense. A 故 is a cause, the settled reason that makes something happen. From that single root, the other senses fan out. Because a cause is something prior, something already established, 故 slides into "old, former, what was the case before." Because 攵 marks deliberate action upon something, 故 takes on "to do on purpose, deliberately." Used as a connective in classical prose, 故 means "for this reason, hence, therefore," literally pointing back to the cause just stated. And in the gentlest extension, the deceased are 故 because they belong now to what has passed.

The radical 攵 (also written 攴, called 反文旁 fǎnwénpáng in everyday Chinese because it resembles a reversed 文) appears in a large family of characters involving action, governance, or striking: 教 jiào (to teach), 政 zhèng (governance), 改 gǎi (to change), 攻 gōng (to attack), 收 shōu (to gather in). In each, something is acted upon. Within 故, the radical reminds us that a cause is not a passive fact but a force that makes things happen. In Japanese, 故 (ko in Sino-Japanese reading) survives in 故郷 (kokyō, hometown) and as the prefix 故 before a deceased person's name, exactly as in Chinese 故 plus a name.

用法yòngfǎFive Senses of 故: One Glyph, Five Threads
故 · the five families of meaning
缘故 yuángù → cause, reason : the settled why behind an event
故乡 gùxiāng → old, former : the place or person belonging to before
故意 gùyì → deliberately : an act done on purpose, with intent
故 (literary) → therefore, hence : a connective pointing back to the cause
病故 bìnggù → deceased : one who has passed, now belonging to the past
All five flow from the Shuo Wen root, a settled cause. A cause is prior, so 故 means old; a cause can be willed, so 故 means deliberate; a cause can be pointed back to, so 故 means therefore; and the dead are those whose lives are now wholly prior, wholly cause and never again effect.
缘故yuángùReason & Cause: Why a Thing Happens or Fails
故 as cause , the root that holds the rest together

The oldest and most literal sense of 故 is cause. When you ask 什么缘故 (shénme yuángù, for what reason) you are asking for the 故 behind an event, the thing that made it be. This sense survives intact in modern compounds: a malfunction is a 故障 (gùzhàng), literally an obstruction with a cause; an accident is a 事故 (shìgù), an incident with a cause behind it. The cause sense is also why 故 anchors the fixed phrase 无缘无故 (wú yuán wú gù), "without cause, for no reason at all," used when something happens with no traceable trigger.

缘故yuángùreason; cause; the why behind something
N 名词
缘 (yuán, cause, affinity, the edge along which things connect) + 故 (cause, reason). A two-character word for the reason behind an event, slightly more formal and reflective than the everyday 原因 (yuányīn). 缘故 often appears at the end of a clause: 因为天气的缘故 (because of the weather), 不知什么缘故 (for some unknown reason). The 缘 lends a faint sense of circumstance or fate, as if the reason were woven into how things were bound to connect.
不知什么缘故，他今天没来上班。
Bùzhī shénme yuángù, tā jīntiān méi lái shàngbān.
For some unknown reason, he didn't come to work today.
因为身体的缘故，她推掉了这次旅行。
Yīnwèi shēntǐ de yuángù, tā tuīdiào le zhè cì lǚxíng.
On account of her health, she called off this trip.
辨析 biànxī · 缘故 vs 原因 原因 (yuányīn) is the neutral, technical word for cause, the one used in science, law, and reports. 缘故 is softer and more conversational, often gestures at circumstance rather than a precise mechanism, and tends to sit at the end of a clause introduced by 因为 or 由于. You write 事故原因 (the cause of the accident) in a report, but say 因为某种缘故 (for a certain reason) when you would rather not spell it out.
故障gùzhàngmalfunction; breakdown; fault
N 名词
故 (cause; here, an underlying fault) + 障 (zhàng, obstruction, barrier). A malfunction in a machine or system: something with a cause behind it that obstructs normal operation. 出故障 (chū gùzhàng) means "to develop a fault." The word is everywhere in modern technical life: 系统故障 (system failure), 机械故障 (mechanical breakdown), 故障代码 (fault code). The 故 here is the diagnostic sense, the reason hidden inside the failure that an engineer must trace.
电梯出故障了，我们只能走楼梯。
Diàntī chū gùzhàng le, wǒmen zhǐ néng zǒu lóutī.
The elevator broke down, so we had to take the stairs.
技术人员很快就找到了故障的原因。
Jìshù rényuán hěn kuài jiù zhǎodào le gùzhàng de yuányīn.
The technicians quickly located the cause of the malfunction.
事故shìgùaccident; incident; mishap
N 名词
事 (shì, matter, affair, event) + 故 (cause, the reason behind it). An accident or untoward incident: literally an event with a cause, usually an unwanted one. 交通事故 (jiāotōng shìgù) is a traffic accident; 安全事故 (ānquán shìgù) is a safety incident. The word frames the event as something to be investigated, traced back to its 故. Compare the unrelated colloquial idiom 没事故 in speech, where people simply say 没事 (méi shì, it's nothing); 事故 proper always implies a reportable mishap.
高速公路上发生了一起严重的交通事故。
Gāosù gōnglù shàng fāshēng le yì qǐ yánzhòng de jiāotōng shìgù.
A serious traffic accident occurred on the expressway.
工厂多年来没有发生过任何安全事故。
Gōngchǎng duōnián lái méiyǒu fāshēng guò rènhé ānquán shìgù.
The factory has not had a single safety incident in many years.
故乡gùxiāngOld & Former: Hometown, Story, Old Friend
故 as the old and the prior , where the warmth lives

Because a cause is something prior, 故 readily means "old, former, belonging to before." This is the most emotionally loaded family of the character. 故乡 (gùxiāng, hometown) is the native place one comes from and longs to return to, a word saturated with the literary tradition of homesickness. 故人 (gùrén) is an old friend from a former time. 故事 (gùshì) is a story, literally "old matters," the things that were, retold. And 故宫 (Gùgōng), the Old Palace, is the formal name of the Forbidden City in Beijing, the former imperial residence. In this family 故 overlaps closely with 旧 (jiù, old, worn): 故 leans toward "former, of a prior time," while 旧 leans toward "worn with use." A friend known long ago is 故人; a worn-out coat is 旧.

故乡gùxiānghometown; native place; homeland
N 名词
故 (old, former) + 乡 (xiāng, countryside, native village). The place one is from, charged with the weight of belonging and longing. 故乡 is the literary and emotional register; the plainer 家乡 (jiāxiāng) is more neutral and everyday. Centuries of poetry rest on this word: the moon seen from a foreign place that calls the wanderer's heart back to the 故乡. To say 离开故乡 (to leave one's hometown) is to invoke the whole tradition of separation and return.
每逢佳节，他都格外思念故乡。
Měi féng jiājié, tā dōu géwài sīniàn gùxiāng.
Every festival, he misses his hometown all the more keenly.
离开故乡多年，他乡音却一点没变。
Líkāi gùxiāng duō nián, tā xiāngyīn què yìdiǎn méi biàn.
Though he left his hometown years ago, his accent hasn't changed at all.
故事gùshìstory; tale; narrative
N 名词
故 (old, former) + 事 (shì, matter, affair, event). A story is literally "old matters," the things that happened, told again. 故事 is the everyday word for any story, whether a children's tale, a film plot, or a real-life account: 讲故事 (jiǎng gùshì, to tell a story), 童话故事 (children's fairy tales), 真实故事 (a true story). The character pairing reveals the Chinese intuition that narrative is the retelling of what was, the past made into shape and handed forward. See the companion entry 事 for the wider family of "matter" words.
奶奶每天晚上都给我讲一个故事。
Nǎinai měitiān wǎnshang dōu gěi wǒ jiǎng yí gè gùshì.
Grandma tells me a story every evening.
这部电影根据一个真实的故事改编。
Zhè bù diànyǐng gēnjù yí gè zhēnshí de gùshì gǎibiān.
This film is adapted from a true story.
故人gùrénold friend; an acquaintance from former days
N 名词
故 (old, former) + 人 (rén, person). An old friend, specifically one known from an earlier period of life, now perhaps far away or long unseen. 故人 belongs to the literary and poetic register, common in classical verse about parting and reunion; the everyday equivalent is 老朋友 (lǎo péngyou). The same 故 appears in 故旧 (gùjiù, old friends and associates). Note that 故人 can also be a delicate way to refer to someone who has died, drawing on the deceased sense of 故.
在异乡偶遇故人，他激动得说不出话。
Zài yìxiāng ǒuyù gùrén, tā jīdòng de shuō bu chū huà.
Running into an old friend in a foreign place, he was too moved to speak.
西出阳关无故人，是古诗里写离别的名句。
Xī chū Yángguān wú gùrén, shì gǔ shī lǐ xiě líbié de míngjù.
"West of Yang Pass there will be no old friends" is a famous classical line about parting.
故宫Gùgōngthe Forbidden City; the Old Palace
N 名词
故 (old, former) + 宫 (gōng, palace). Literally "the Old Palace," the formal Chinese name for the former imperial palace complex in Beijing, known in English as the Forbidden City. It was the residence of emperors of the Ming and Qing dynasties and is now the Palace Museum (故宫博物院, Gùgōng Bówùyuàn). The name itself encodes the historical shift: it became 故宫, the former palace, once it ceased to be the seat of imperial power. The 故 marks it as belonging to a closed chapter, preserved and visited rather than inhabited.
我们计划明天去故宫参观。
Wǒmen jìhuà míngtiān qù Gùgōng cānguān.
We plan to visit the Forbidden City tomorrow.
故宫的红墙金瓦在阳光下格外壮观。
Gùgōng de hóng qiáng jīn wǎ zài yángguāng xià géwài zhuàngguān.
The red walls and golden tiles of the Forbidden City are especially magnificent in the sunlight.
故意gùyìDeliberately & Hence: On Purpose, and the Literary Therefore
故 as will and as connective , the strike radical comes forward

Two more senses round out the character. First, deliberateness: when 攵, the strike radical, brings its sense of willed action to the fore, 故 means "on purpose, deliberately." 故意 (gùyì) is the everyday adverb for doing something intentionally, and it carries a faint negative charge, since one usually accuses another of doing harm 故意 rather than praising a kindness done 故意. Second, the connective: in classical and formal modern prose, a lone 故 means "therefore, hence," pointing back to a cause just stated. This is the cause sense turned into grammar. A classical argument runs 因为A，故B (because A, therefore B). The literary 故 still surfaces in formal writing, legal language, and set phrases such as 故曰 (gù yuē, hence it is said).

故意gùyìdeliberately; on purpose; intentionally
Adj 形容词
故 (deliberate) + 意 (yì, intention, will, mind). To do something on purpose, with conscious intent. Used adverbially: 他故意迟到 (he was late on purpose), 不是故意的 (didn't mean to, it wasn't intentional). 故意 most often colors an action as deliberately unkind or provocative, so 不是故意的 is the standard apology for an accidental slight. In legal language 故意 (intent) contrasts sharply with 过失 (guòshī, negligence), and the distinction can decide a verdict.
对不起，我不是故意的。
Duìbuqǐ, wǒ búshì gùyì de.
Sorry, I didn't do it on purpose.
他故意把话说得含糊，让人摸不清他的意思。
Tā gùyì bǎ huà shuō de hánhú, ràng rén mō bu qīng tā de yìsi.
He deliberately spoke vaguely, leaving people unable to grasp his meaning.
辨析 biànxī · 故意 vs 特意 故意 (gùyì) and 特意 (tèyì) both mean "on purpose," but their feeling is opposite. 故意 usually marks an act done to annoy, provoke, or deceive, so it carries a negative tilt: 他故意气我 (he provoked me on purpose). 特意 means "specially, going out of one's way," and is warm or neutral: 我特意为你做了这道菜 (I made this dish specially for you). Swap them and the tone flips: 特意 frames effort as a gift, 故意 frames intent as a fault.
故gùtherefore; hence; for this reason (literary)
Conj 连词
A lone 故 in classical and formal prose works as a connective: "therefore, hence." It points back to the cause just stated, the cause sense of the character turned into a logical hinge. The pattern is 因为A，故B (because A, hence B), or simply 故 at the head of a concluding clause. It belongs to written and formal register; in speech one says 所以 (suǒyǐ) instead. The literary 故 survives in fixed expressions like 故曰 (hence it is said) and in the measured prose of essays, contracts, and classical commentary.
天行有常，不为尧存，不为桀亡，故应之以治则吉。
Tiān xíng yǒu cháng, bù wèi Yáo cún, bù wèi Jié wáng, gù yìng zhī yǐ zhì zé jí.
Heaven's course is constant, not preserved for the sage-king nor abolished for the tyrant; therefore meeting it with good order brings fortune. (after Xunzi)
证据不足，故本案不予立案。
Zhèngjù bùzú, gù běn àn bù yǔ lì'àn.
The evidence being insufficient, this case is therefore not accepted for filing.
病故bìnggùto die of illness; to pass away from sickness
V 动词
病 (bìng, illness) + 故 (here, to pass away). A measured, somewhat formal way to say that someone died of illness, used in obituaries, official notices, and dignified speech. The 故 draws on the deceased sense: the dead belong now to what has passed. The related 已故 (yǐgù, the late, deceased) is placed before a name, as in 已故的张教授 (the late Professor Zhang). Plain 去世 (qùshì) is the common neutral term for "to pass away"; 病故 specifies the cause as illness.
老人去年因病故于家中，享年八十九岁。
Lǎorén qùnián yīn bìng gù yú jiā zhōng, xiǎngnián bāshíjiǔ suì.
The old man passed away from illness at home last year, at the age of eighty-nine.
这是已故作家留下的最后一部手稿。
Zhè shì yǐgù zuòjiā liúxià de zuìhòu yí bù shǒugǎo.
This is the last manuscript left behind by the late writer.
成语chéngyǔIdioms & Set Phrases
温故知新 wēn gù zhī xīn review the old to learn the new: deepening understanding by returning to what is known 温 (wēn, to warm, to review, as one reheats food) + 故 (the old, what was learned before) + 知 (zhī, to know) + 新 (xīn, the new). From the Analects of Confucius, Chapter 2.11: 温故而知新，可以为师矣, "He who reviews the old and discerns the new is fit to be a teacher." The Confucian theory of learning in four characters: mastery is not piling up fresh information but returning to foundational material and finding new meaning each time. The 故 here is the old sense of the character, the prior knowledge one warms over again. Still cited constantly in education and self-cultivation.
一见如故 yī jiàn rú gù to feel like old friends at first meeting: instant rapport 一 (yī, one) + 见 (jiàn, to meet) + 如 (rú, as if, like) + 故 (an old friend, drawing on the 故人 sense). To meet someone for the first time and feel as though you have known them for years. The phrase captures that rare warmth of immediate kinship, where strangers talk freely from the first moment as if resuming an old acquaintance. 他们一见如故，聊了整整一夜 (they hit it off at once and talked the whole night through). The 故 carries the full weight of "old friend," so the idiom collapses the distance between first sight and long friendship.
明知故犯 míng zhī gù fàn to knowingly do wrong: to break a rule while fully aware it is wrong 明 (míng, clearly) + 知 (zhī, to know) + 故 (deliberately, the willed-action sense) + 犯 (fàn, to violate, to offend). To commit an offense while clearly knowing it is forbidden, the opposite of an honest mistake. The 故 here is the deliberate sense: not only did the person know the rule, they broke it on purpose. The idiom is a sharp reproach, common in moral and legal contexts: 明知故犯，罪加一等 (to offend knowingly compounds the guilt). It pairs the knowing (知) with the willing (故) to mark the offense as fully culpable.
故弄玄虚 gù nòng xuán xū to deliberately mystify: to make something seem more obscure or profound than it is 故 (deliberately) + 弄 (nòng, to play with, to manipulate) + 玄 (xuán, mysterious, dark, profound) + 虚 (xū, empty, unreal). To purposely dress something up as mysterious in order to confuse or impress, when in fact there is little substance behind it. The 故 is again the deliberate sense, marking the mystification as a calculated act. Used to criticize people who hide simple matters behind smoke: 别故弄玄虚，有话直说 (stop being deliberately mysterious, just say it plainly). 玄虚 by itself means a deceptive show of depth.
记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image

Picture a hand with a rod tapping at something old. That is 攵, the strike radical, set beside 古, the ancient. The single image holds the whole character: a deliberate hand acting upon what is already old. From that one picture the five senses unfold like spokes. The thing the hand acts upon is a cause (缘故). What it taps is old and former (故乡, 故人, 故事). The hand acts on purpose (故意). Trace the rod back along the strike and you find the reason, so 故 also means therefore (因为A，故B). And the oldest things of all, those wholly past, are the dead (病故, 已故).

The discipline for a learner is to feel which spoke is active in a given compound. In 故乡 and 故事 the warmth of the former rules; the home and the tale are cherished, not blamed. In 故意 and 故弄玄虚 the willed hand comes forward, usually with a critical edge. In 故障 and 事故 the hidden cause is what matters, the fault to be traced. And the lone literary 故 is pure logic, the cause turned into a hinge between premise and conclusion.

Hold 故 against its near neighbor 旧. Both reach into the past, but 故 is the former, the prior, the cause-that-was, while 旧 is the worn, the used, the thing aged by handling. An old friend known long ago is a 故人; a coat worn thin is 旧. 温故知新 uses 故 because the old here is prior knowledge to be warmed over, not a worn object. When you meet 故, ask which thread the context has pulled: the cause behind, the time before, the will within, the hence that follows, or the life now wholly past.
