字源zìyuánEtymology & Structure
字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight

苦 is a tidy pictophonetic: 苦 = 艹 + 古. The grass radical 艹 (cǎo) sits on top and carries the meaning, marking the word as a plant; 古 (gǔ) sits below and carries the sound, supplying the near-rhyme that gave kǔ its reading. The whole was originally the name of a specific bitter herb, a wild plant you would spit out the moment it touched your tongue.

The Shuōwén Jiězì glosses 苦 as 大苦, identifying it with a bitter medicinal plant. The seal form already shows the three-stalk grass crown above the 古 element, the same stacked arrangement that survives in the modern square. Nothing pictographic happens in the lower half: 古 is there for its voice, not its picture of an old mouth.

From the taste, the sense reached outward by an obvious bridge. A bitter herb is unpleasant to swallow; an unpleasant life is bitter to live. So 苦 stretched from flavor to hardship (苦难 kǔnàn, suffering), then to the effort that hardship demands (刻苦 kèkǔ, painstaking), and finally to the adverb that colors a verb with grim persistence: 苦练 (kǔliàn) is to train bitterly, to drill until it hurts.

Buddhism deepened the word permanently. When the First Noble Truth was rendered into Chinese, 苦 (kǔ) became the standard translation of Sanskrit duḥkha, the pervasive unsatisfactoriness of existence. To Chinese ears, the doctrine that 人生是苦 (rénshēng shì kǔ), life is suffering, lands with the directness of a flavor you already know.

辛劳xīnláoToil & Effort
辛苦xīnkǔhard, toilsome; thanks for your trouble
Adj 形容词 xíngróngcí
辛 (xīn), pungent or laborious, pairs with 苦 (kǔ), bitter, to name effort that wears you down. As a set phrase it doubles as warm thanks: telling someone 辛苦了 acknowledges the toil they just put in for you.
这份工作很辛苦，但我喜欢。
Zhè fèn gōngzuò hěn xīnkǔ, dàn wǒ xǐhuan.
This job is hard, but I like it.
大家辛苦了，先休息一下吧。
Dàjiā xīnkǔ le, xiān xiūxi yíxià ba.
Thanks for your hard work, everyone, let us rest a moment.
辨析 biànxī · 辛苦 vs. 痛苦 辛苦 is physical toil and fatigue; 痛苦 (tòngkǔ) is inner pain or anguish. A long workday is 辛苦; a grief is 痛苦.
吃苦chīkǔto endure hardship; to bear suffering
V 动词 dòngcí
Literally to eat bitterness: 吃 (chī) takes 苦 as its object, treating hardship as something swallowed. A core virtue word, the capacity to 吃苦 is praised as the backbone of anyone who endures and gets ahead.
年轻人应该多吃点苦。
Niánqīng rén yīnggāi duō chī diǎn kǔ.
Young people ought to endure a bit more hardship.
苦力kǔlìhard manual labor; a coolie
N 名词 míngcí
苦 (bitter, hard) plus 力 (lì, strength) names grueling physical labor and, historically, the laborer himself. The English word "coolie" carries this same syllable. In modern speech 干苦力 means to do back-breaking work.
他年轻时干过苦力。
Tā niánqīng shí gànguò kǔlì.
In his youth he did hard manual labor.
痛楚tòngchǔPain & Suffering
痛苦tòngkǔpain, agony, suffering
Adj 形容词 xíngróngcí
痛 (tòng), to ache, joins 苦 (kǔ), bitter, to name suffering felt in body or mind. The most direct word for anguish, it covers physical pain and emotional torment alike.
失去亲人是巨大的痛苦。
Shīqù qīnrén shì jùdà de tòngkǔ.
Losing a loved one is an immense suffering.
他痛苦地闭上了眼睛。
Tā tòngkǔ de bìshàng le yǎnjing.
He shut his eyes in pain.
苦难kǔnànsuffering, tribulation, misery
N 名词 míngcí
苦 (bitter) joins 难 (nàn, calamity, disaster) to name suffering on a large, often historical scale: famine, war, oppression. Weightier than everyday hardship, 苦难 belongs to the language of nations and generations.
这个民族经历了深重的苦难。
Zhège mínzú jīnglì le shēnzhòng de kǔnàn.
This people endured profound suffering.
辨析 biànxī · 苦难 vs. 困难 苦难 (kǔnàn) is suffering and misery; 困难 (kùnnan) is a difficulty or obstacle to be solved. A war brings 苦难; a hard exam is a 困难.
苦衷kǔzhōnghidden hardship; unspoken difficulty
N 名词 míngcí
苦 (bitter) plus 衷 (zhōng, inner feelings) names the private difficulty behind a person's puzzling choice, the bitterness they cannot say aloud. To have a 苦衷 is to act badly for a reason you keep hidden.
他这么做也有他的苦衷。
Tā zhème zuò yě yǒu tā de kǔzhōng.
He had his own hidden reasons for doing this.
勤勉qínmiǎnDiligence & Painstaking Effort
刻苦kèkǔhardworking, painstaking, assiduous
Adj 形容词 xíngróngcí
刻 (kè), to carve or engrave, joins 苦 (kǔ) to describe effort that bites in: study or training pursued through self-denial. A 刻苦 student drills past comfort, the way a blade is sharpened against stone.
她学习非常刻苦。
Tā xuéxí fēicháng kèkǔ.
She studies extremely hard.
苦练kǔliànto train hard, to drill relentlessly
V 动词 dòngcí
Here 苦 works as an adverb on 练 (liàn, to train): to practice bitterly, to grind. The word frames mastery as something earned through painful repetition rather than talent.
他每天苦练书法。
Tā měitiān kǔliàn shūfǎ.
He drills at calligraphy hard every day.
苦心kǔxīnpainstaking effort; careful pains
N 名词 míngcí
苦 (bitter) plus 心 (xīn, heart, mind) names the trouble one's heart goes through for someone else's sake. 一片苦心 (yí piàn kǔxīn) is a heartful of well-meant pains, often unrecognized.
父母的苦心，孩子未必能懂。
Fùmǔ de kǔxīn, háizi wèibì néng dǒng.
A parent's painstaking care is not always understood by the child.
成语chéngyǔIdioms & Set Phrases
苦尽甘来kǔ jìn gān láibitterness ends, sweetness comes: hardship gives way to better days苦 (bitter) and 甘 (sweet) frame a full turn of fortune. The standard consolation offered to anyone enduring a long hard stretch; the reward is built into the saying.
良药苦口liáng yào kǔ kǒugood medicine is bitter to the mouth: honest advice is hard to hearFrom the Kǒngzǐ Jiāyǔ. Often completed by 忠言逆耳 (loyal words grate on the ear). Used to soften unwelcome but useful criticism.
苦口婆心kǔ kǒu pó xīnbitter mouth, grandmotherly heart: to urge earnestly and patientlyDescribes someone who repeats hard advice tirelessly out of genuine care, the way an old woman nags her grandchild for their own good.
含辛茹苦hán xīn rú kǔto hold pungency and swallow bitterness: to endure every hardship茹 (rú) means to eat. Almost always said of parents, especially mothers, who suffer silently for years to raise their children.
记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image

Picture a bowl of dark herbal medicine cooling on the table, a clump of wild grass still floating in it. You raise it to your lips, and the taste is so bitter your face puckers. That clump of grass is the radical 艹 sitting on top of 苦; the bowl itself, old and well-used, is 古 (gǔ), the ancient sound underneath. Grass over old: a bitter old remedy.

Now follow the bitterness outward. The same drink that pinches your tongue is what 良药苦口 promises: good medicine tastes bad. Every compound on this page is some version of that swallow. 吃苦 (chīkǔ) is choosing to drink it; 刻苦 (kèkǔ) is drinking it daily until it sharpens you; 痛苦 (tòngkǔ) is when the bitterness reaches the heart, not just the tongue.

Hold the last note: 苦尽甘来 (kǔ jìn gān lái). You finish the bitter cup, and only then does the sweet arrive. Whenever you meet 苦, taste the grass-over-古 bitterness first, then remember it is the kind that, drunk to the bottom, turns sweet.
