字源 zìyuán Etymology & Structure
字源洞见 zìyuán dòngjiàn · Etymological Insight

子 zǐ is a pictograph of a swaddled infant. The oracle-bone and bronze forms show a baby with a large round head, arms either raised or wrapped, and the lower body bound in cloth so the legs read as a single stroke. The modern shape preserves this exactly: the top is the head, the long hook is the swaddled body, and the horizontal stroke across the middle is the bundled arms. As a structure the Unihan decomposition reads 子 = ⿻ 了 (liǎo, the hooked body) overlaid with 一 (yī, the cross-stroke of the arms).

From this one image the character spreads into four senses that anchor thousands of words:

1. Child, offspring. The literal meaning. 孩子 háizi (child), 儿子 érzi (son), 子女 zǐnǚ (sons and daughters, i.e. children collectively).
2. Seed, the small generative thing. What a plant or a process produces: 种子 zhǒngzi (seed), 瓜子 guāzǐ (melon seeds), 原子 yuánzǐ (atom, the "original seed").
3. Master, the honorific. Appended to a surname it means "the Master," the respectful title for a teacher or philosopher: 孔子 Kǒngzǐ (Confucius), 孟子 Mèngzǐ (Mencius), 君子 jūnzǐ (the noble person).
4. The empty noun-suffix. Toneless 子 (zi) attaches to a root to mark it as a concrete noun: 桌子 zhuōzi (table), 房子 fángzi (house), 杯子 bēizi (cup). Here 子 has bleached out almost all meaning and survives only as a grammatical tag.

子 is also the first of the twelve Earthly Branches (地支), where it names the hour around midnight and the year of the Rat. The thread running through every sense is smallness and generation: the infant, the seed, the disciple who carries a teacher forward, the bare noun waiting to be made concrete.

孩子 háizi Child & Offspring
孩子 háizi child; kid; one's children
N 名词 míngcí
孩 hái (child) + 子 zi (suffix). The everyday word for a child, whether your own or any youngster. Used affectionately of adults too: 你还是个孩子 "you're still a kid."
他们有两个孩子。
Tāmen yǒu liǎng ge háizi.
They have two children.
这个孩子很聪明。
Zhège háizi hěn cōngming.
This child is very clever.
儿子 érzi son
N 名词 míngcí
儿 ér (child, son) + 子 zi (suffix). The standard word for a son. Its pair is 女儿 nǚ'ér (daughter), which keeps 儿 but not 子.
他的儿子在上大学。
Tā de érzi zài shàng dàxué.
His son is in college.
子女 zǐnǚ sons and daughters; children (collectively)
N 名词 míngcí
子 zǐ (sons) + 女 nǚ (daughters). A formal, collective term for one's children as a group, common in writing about family and law. Here 子 keeps its full third tone.
父母应该照顾子女。
Fùmǔ yīnggāi zhàogù zǐnǚ.
Parents should care for their children.
辨析 biànxī · 孩子 vs. 子女 孩子 is warm and everyday, and can mean a single child or "kids" in general. 子女 is formal and always collective: it frames children as offspring in a family or legal sense, never used to address a child directly.
种子 zhǒngzi seed (of a plant); (sports) seeded player
N 名词 míngcí
种 zhǒng (kind; to sow) + 子 zi. The seed sense of 子 made explicit. By extension it names a "seeded" competitor in a tournament, the one expected to grow into a winner.
把种子种在土里。
Bǎ zhǒngzi zhòng zài tǔ lǐ.
Plant the seeds in the soil.
君子 jūnzǐ Master & Gentleman — the Honorific 子
语义洞见 yǔyì dòngjiàn · Semantic Insight

The honorific 子 is how classical Chinese names its teachers. Appended to a surname it means "the Master": 孔子 Kǒngzǐ is "Master Kong," whom the West calls Confucius; 孟子 Mèngzǐ is "Master Meng," Mencius; 老子 Lǎozǐ is the "Old Master." The same 子 marks the disciple who carries the teaching onward: 弟子 dìzǐ (disciple), 子弟 zǐdì (the young men of a family or community).

From the title grew an idea of character. 君子 jūnzǐ began as "the ruler's son," the man of the noble class, and Confucius reforged it to mean something earned rather than inherited: the person of cultivated virtue, the moral exemplar. Its foil is 小人 xiǎorén, the petty person. The contrast between 君子 and 小人 runs through the whole Analects, and it remains the everyday Chinese yardstick for whether someone acts honorably.

君子 jūnzǐ a person of noble character; gentleman; the morally cultivated
N 名词 míngcí
君 jūn (lord; ruler) + 子 zǐ. Once "the lord's son," remade by Confucius into the ideal of earned virtue. The benchmark of honorable conduct, set against 小人 xiǎorén, the petty person.
君子坦荡荡。
Jūnzǐ tǎn dàngdàng.
The noble person is open and at ease. (Analects)
弟子 dìzǐ disciple; student of a master
N 名词 míngcí
弟 dì (younger brother) + 子 zǐ. The pupil who studies under a teacher and carries the lineage forward, used of philosophical, martial, and religious schools alike.
孔子有三千弟子。
Kǒngzǐ yǒu sānqiān dìzǐ.
Confucius had three thousand disciples.
妻子 qīzi wife
N 名词 míngcí
妻 qī (wife) + 子 zi (suffix). The standard modern word for wife. Note the tone: in classical Chinese 妻子 qīzǐ meant "wife and children," but the modern toneless reading qīzi means the wife alone.
他的妻子是医生。
Tā de qīzi shì yīshēng.
His wife is a doctor.
词尾 cíwěi The Noun-Suffix — Toneless 子 (zi)
语法洞见 yǔfǎ dòngjiàn · Grammatical Insight

The busiest job 子 does in modern Chinese is the one where it means almost nothing. Toneless 子 (zi) attaches to a one-syllable root and turns it into a stable, concrete noun: 桌子 zhuōzi (table), 椅子 yǐzi (chair), 房子 fángzi (house), 杯子 bēizi (cup), 鞋子 xiézi (shoes), 帽子 màozi (hat), 本子 běnzi (notebook).

Why does the suffix exist at all? Many of these roots are single syllables that feel incomplete on their own, and Mandarin prefers two-syllable nouns. 子 fills the second slot and signals "this is a thing." It is one of three such noun-suffixes: 子 (zi), 儿 (r, the northern diminutive), and 头 (tou). The choice between them is largely fixed by convention rather than meaning, so the suffix is learned word by word.

Listen for the tone. When 子 is a full word it keeps third tone (zǐ: 君子, 种子, 子女). When it is the bare suffix it goes neutral (zi: 桌子, 孩子, 妻子). The tone is the clearest signal of which 子 you are hearing.

房子 fángzi house; building
N 名词 míngcí
房 fáng (room; house) + 子 zi. A house or building as a physical structure. For "home" in the emotional sense, Chinese uses 家 jiā instead.
他们买了一套房子。
Tāmen mǎi le yī tào fángzi.
They bought a house.
桌子 zhuōzi table; desk
N 名词 míngcí
桌 zhuō (table) + 子 zi. A textbook example of the bare suffix: the root 桌 rarely stands alone, and 子 makes it a usable noun.
把书放在桌子上。
Bǎ shū fàng zài zhuōzi shàng.
Put the book on the table.
样子 yàngzi appearance; look; manner; the way something seems
N 名词 míngcí
样 yàng (shape; kind) + 子 zi. The look or manner of someone or something. Also idiomatic: 看样子 kàn yàngzi "it looks like / from the looks of it."
看样子要下雨了。
Kàn yàngzi yào xià yǔ le.
It looks like it's going to rain.
成语 chéngyǔ Idioms & Set Phrases
君子之交 jūnzǐ zhī jiāo the friendship of noble people: plain as water, lasting and unselfish Lit: noble-person's friendship. From the saying 君子之交淡如水, "the friendship of the noble is bland as water," meaning a true bond needs no flattery or gifts and so never sours.
望子成龙 wàng zǐ chéng lóng to hope one's son becomes a dragon: to long for a child's great success Lit: hope-son-become-dragon. The dragon is the emblem of supreme achievement, so the phrase captures every parent's wish that a child will rise high. Its pair is 望女成凤, hoping a daughter becomes a phoenix.
天之骄子 tiān zhī jiāo zǐ a favored child of Heaven: one singularly blessed by fortune Lit: Heaven's-spoiled-child. Originally said of the Xiongnu nomads who boasted Heaven's favor; now used of anyone who seems destined for success, sometimes with a touch of envy.
桃李满天下 táo lǐ mǎn tiānxià peaches and plums fill the world: a teacher's students are everywhere Lit: peach-plum-fill-the-world. 桃李 (peach and plum trees) stand for a teacher's 弟子, the disciples cultivated over a lifetime. To say someone's 桃李满天下 is high praise for a great educator.
记忆法 jìyìfǎ · Master Retention Image

Picture the swaddled infant at the center of 子, then watch it grow outward. The baby is the literal child (孩子, 儿子). Make it tiny and generative and it becomes a seed (种子). Let it grow up under a teacher and it becomes a disciple, and the teacher a 子, a Master (孔子, 君子). Empty it of all meaning and it becomes the bare tag on a noun (桌子, 房子).

The tone is your guide back. A full-tone zǐ is always one of the meaningful 子: child, seed, or Master. A toneless zi is the suffix that means little more than "this is a thing." Hear which one, and you know which 子 you are looking at.
